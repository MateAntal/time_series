    Session 10  ·  date and room in this session's announcements

    Twenty questions, eighty minutes, one calculator.

    • 13 concept questions, four options each. Say what a tool is for and what its output means. Nothing asks you to write or read R.
    • 7 calculation questions, five options each. Short calculations by hand, most of them on one small quarterly table: the steps of a classical decomposition, moving averages, benchmark forecasts. Round every step to two decimals.
    • Bring a non-programmable, non-graphing calculator. No phones, watches or other devices. Answers go on a separate answer sheet, one letter per question.
    There is no retake. If you cannot attend for personal or work reasons, the final carries 60% instead of 30% (see the syllabus).

    Wrong answers cost nothing, so answer every question.

    Being able to type a function call from memory earns nothing on this paper. Being able to say why a 2×12-MA spans thirteen points, and to compute one by hand, earns a great deal.

    The calculations use only addition, subtraction and division. A basic calculator is all you need; practice with the one you will bring.

    Coverage

    Sessions 1 to 8, on paper.

    On the exam

    The stochastic-process framing, graphics and the ACF, the whole decomposition block (classical decomposition by hand as well as in R, and STL), benchmarks and fitted values, forecasting through a decomposition.

    Not on the exam

    Session 9's residual diagnostics, and everything from Session 11 on. All of it is examined in the final, which covers the whole course.

    • Practice the hand calculations before the day. R did them for you in class. Take a few rows from the notebooks and redo them on paper: a 2×4-MA from 06_B, each step of the classical decomposition in 07_D, a benchmark forecast from 08_A. Then check every number against R's output.
    • Use the calculator you will bring, and round every step to two decimals, as the exam asks.

    If you did the homework yourself, the only new thing on the paper is doing by hand what R did for you.

    Session 9 is deferred, not dropped: the final covers the whole course, Sessions 1 to 19, and Group Assignment 2 depends on Session 9.

    Redoing a few rows is enough. The exam's calculations follow the same steps on a small table; what it tests is whether you know which numbers to add, subtract and divide.

    One line per session

    For every line: can you explain it, and can you compute it by hand?

    ExplainCompute by hand
    S1R, RStudio, projects, packages: the tools, not examined on paper.
    S2A time series as a stochastic process: each yt one realization of Yt. Tsibbles, index and key.
    S3Time, seasonal, subseries and scatterplots. Trend vs seasonality vs cycle. What a correlation coefficient misses.
    S4Lag plots and the ACF; how trend and seasonality show in a correlogram; white noise.the bounds ±2/√T
    S5Additive vs multiplicative; the ladder, square root, cube root, log, inverse, weakest to strongest; detrended vs seasonally adjusted.yt - Tt and yt - St
    S6Centered moving averages; odd m vs the 2×m construction; why the ends are missing.a 2×m-MA and a 3×3-MA; the values missing at each end
    S7Classical decomposition in four steps, and how one outlier spreads through them; the two criteria; STL and its two windows.the seasonal component: average by quarter, then center
    S8Fitted values vs forecasts; residuals exist only for fitted values; four benchmarks; forecasting through a decomposition.each benchmark's forecast from a table

    Mark the lines you cannot explain out loud or work on paper. Those are your questions.

    Three things cause trouble every year, and they are worth checking first: the difference between seasonality and a cycle (Session 3), why an even seasonal period needs the 2×m construction (Session 6), and the difference between a fitted value and a forecast (Session 8). If you are solid on those three, the rest tends to follow.

    Session 11

    After the exam, the course picks back up.

    Session 11 is transformations that even out a series' variance: logarithms, powers, and the Box–Cox family.

    • It builds on Session 5's transformation ladder: square root, cube root, log, inverse.
    • It is the first fix for a funnel. A residual plot whose spread widens over time, from Session 9, is the symptom it treats.

    Session 5 chose a transformation by eye. Session 11 chooses it with a number, λ.

    Session 11's transformation topic is where Session 9's diagnostics pay off immediately: a funnel-shaped residual plot is the symptom, and the Box–Cox family is the principled remedy.
