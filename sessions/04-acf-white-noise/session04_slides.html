    Session 4. Lag plots, the autocorrelation function, and white noise.

    Where we are

    Last session you named the patterns by eye. Today you measure them.

    • A lag plot asks the question with a scatterplot: does today look like k steps ago?
    • The autocorrelation function compresses each of those scatterplots into one number.
    • White noise is the reference case — the series that remembers nothing.

    One question, asked at increasing distances. Everything on the next twenty-five slides is a different way of asking it.

    This session is fpp3 sections 2.7 to 2.9, and notebook 04_C covers them in the same order.

    Two names for one picture: the book and the notebook both say correlogram and ACF plot, and they mean exactly the same graph. Keep that distinct from the lag plot, which the notebook writes as “lagplot”: the lag plot is the grid of scatterplots, the correlogram is the bar chart.

    Recap from Session 3  ·  recent_beer

    A lag is the same column, moved down.

    The shaded staircase is the whole difficulty of this session in one shape. Every lag costs you a row, and the lagged column is a shorter series than the one it came from.

    Two reasons lags matter, and the notebook lists both. The first is the one we spend today on: relating yt to its own past. The second is relating yt to the past of a different variable — marketing spend in March against sales in June, which is exactly the SOI-and-recruitment example that closed Session 3.

    A trap worth knowing now: loading fpp3 prints dplyr::lag() masks stats::lag(). That masking is what you want, because stats::lag() does not shift a column, it relabels a ts object's time attribute and will silently give you nonsense inside a mutate(). The opposite direction is dplyr::lead().

    Beer production, quarterly  ·  2000 Q1 – 2002 Q2

    Draw the lag on top of the original and watch the two lock together.

    In phase means the scatterplot at that lag will be a tight diagonal. Out of phase means it will be a blob. The ACF is the number that says which.

    Phase language is borrowed from waves, and it is worth keeping because it also tells you what happens half a period out. Shift a four-quarter season by two quarters and the peaks land on the troughs: that is anti-phase, and it produces a strong negative relationship rather than no relationship at all. Slide 9 comes back to this.

    The notebook says this series has “simple seasonality”, meaning the yearly cycle is the only one present. Sub-daily series, as Session 3's electricity example showed, can carry two or three at once, and their lag plots are correspondingly harder to read.

    04_C  ·  gg_lag(y = Beer, geom = "point", lags = 4)

    Forget time. Plot the series against its own past.

    This is Session 3's scatterplot, with one change: both axes are the same series, one of them shifted. You are asking whether the series correlates with itself.

    Left to itself, gg_lag() draws geom = "path" and colors the points by season, which produces a colored tangle. The notebook passes geom = "point" to strip that back to a plain scatterplot, and that is the right call while you are learning to read the shape.

    The faint diagonal in each panel is the 45-degree line y = x, drawn for reference. It is not a regression line, so do not read its slope as a strength — the strength is in how tightly the cloud hugs it.

    04_C  ·  gg_lag(y = Beer, geom = "point", lags = 1:12)

    Twelve scatterplots. They do not just get weaker — they alternate.

    Tight is what matters, not upward. A tight downward cloud is just as much memory as a tight upward one — the series remembers, and the answer is “the opposite of now”.

    This is the picture the beer data actually produces, and the alternation is not an accident. For a season of length m = 4, shifting by 2 — half a period — maps peaks onto troughs, which is the strongest possible negative relationship. Shifting by 1 or 3 lands you a quarter of the way round the cycle, where highs and lows are equally mixed and nothing survives averaging.

    Any seasonal series does this. Monthly data with m = 12 has its negative panels near lags 6, 18, 30. If you ever see a lag plot where the tight panels are only upward, the series has a trend holding everything positive — which is slide 14.

    04_C  ·  reading the grid

    The lag plot finds the period without a formula.

    • Scan for tight panels, in either direction. Note which lag numbers they sit at.
    • If the tight upward panels are at multiples of m, you have found the seasonal period. The tight downward ones will sit halfway between them.
    • If every panel is a blob, the series may have no memory at all. That is the white-noise case, and the ACF settles it with a test.
    It does not scale. Twelve panels is readable. Weekly data needs fifty-two before a yearly season can show up, and a grid that size is unreadable. That limitation is why the ACF exists.
    The panel grid is also awkward for a second reason: each panel throws away the one thing the correlogram keeps, which is a common vertical scale. You are comparing tightness by eye across twelve separate axes.

    So the lag plot is a teaching device and a first look, not a working tool. From here on you will reach for ACF() in practice and come back to gg_lag() only when a correlogram shows you something you want to see as a cloud.

    Autocorrelation vs Pearson's r

    Each lag is computed from a different number of pairs.

    • Pearson's r is defined on a set of pairs, and normalizes by the spread of those pairs. At lag 1 that is 41 pairs; at lag 16 it is 26.
    • So the yardstick changes underneath you. Sixteen coefficients, sixteen slightly different definitions of “average” and “spread”.

    We want one ruler for all sixteen. So we fix the part that measures, and let only the part being measured shrink.

    Why sixteen? ACF() picks its default lag_max as 10 log₁₀(T), rounded down — here 10 log₁₀(42) = 16.2, so sixteen lags, which is exactly the number of rows the printout has. For a keyed tsibble it uses the shortest series.

    If the room wants a picture: autocorrelation measures every lag against one yardstick, nailed to the wall. Pearson's r hands each lag its own yardstick, cut to fit whichever room it happens to be standing in. Only the nailed one lets you say who is taller.

    There is a deeper reason for the fixed denominator than tidiness, and it is the reason every statistics package does it this way. Dividing every lag by the same c₀ guarantees the resulting sequence is a valid autocorrelation function — positive semi-definite, in the jargon — which the pair-by-pair version does not. Models fitted to an invalid ACF can produce negative variances.

    The formula, and the one difference that matters

    Same numerator. Different denominator.

    # Pearson's r — normalizes by the spread of the pairs it used r_k = sum (y_t - ybar)(y_{t-k} - ybar) ------------------------------------------------ sqrt[ sum (y_t - ybar)^2 * sum (y_{t-k} - ybar)^2 ] # Autocorrelation — normalizes by the spread of the whole series r_k = sum_{t=k+1}^{T} (y_t - ybar)(y_{t-k} - ybar) ------------------------------------------------ sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t - ybar)^2
    • Bottom: t = 1 to T. Every lag is divided by the same quantity — the total variation in the series. It does not depend on k.
    • Top: t = k+1 to T. It cannot start earlier, because yt−k is NA until row k+1. So the numerator loses a term for every step back.

    Fixed ruler, shrinking thing being measured. The consequence is deliberate, and it is the whole point of the next slide.

    Written more compactly, rk = ck / c0, where ck is the sample autocovariance at lag k and c0 is the sample variance. Every lag shares one c0. That is the entire content of the slide in five symbols, and it is the form you will meet again in Session 9 and in any ARIMA course.

    Note the mean: ybar is the mean of the whole series in both sums, including in the yt−k factor. It is not the mean of the lagged column. This is another small way the quantity refuses to let k change the yardstick.

    What the fixed denominator buys

    Distant lags are charged a toll, on purpose.

    • Fewer terms on top, the same number underneath. So a relationship of identical strength scores lower at lag 12 than at lag 4.
    • This is the design, not a defect. The notebook is explicit: the coefficient is built to say that the further back a lag is, the less influence it should be credited with.
    • It does not cap anything. A distant lag can still be large — beer scores 0.632 at lag 12. The penalty tilts the scale; it does not impose a ceiling.

    Think of a photograph rather than a jury: everything stays in frame, and things further away are drawn smaller. Nothing is excluded, and you can still tell a mountain from a pebble.

    The comparison to hold on to is that a correlogram is a picture with perspective, not a table of independent measurements. It is why the seasonal spikes at 4, 8, 12 and 16 step down — 0.802, 0.707, 0.632, 0.498 — even though the seasonality behind them is not weakening at all.

    Do not over-read the arithmetic, though. The numerator loses only k terms out of T, so for a long series the penalty is gentle; for a short one it bites. A forty-two point series at lag 16 has lost nearly 40% of its terms, which is another argument for not reading the far right-hand end of a short correlogram too confidently.

    Now we compute

    ACF(), then autoplot()

    • recent_beer |> ACF() — sixteen rows of lag and coefficient
    • recent_beer |> ACF() |> autoplot() — the same sixteen numbers as bars
    • ACF(lag_max = 24) — ask for more
    The output is a tsibble. One column of lags, one of coefficients. You can filter() and arrange() it like any other — useful when you want the largest coefficient rather than a picture.
    The index column has type <cf_lag> and it prints as 4Q rather than 4, because it carries the interval of the original series. That is a feature: a correlogram of quarterly data and one of monthly data cannot be silently compared, and the unit on the axis is what stops you.

    It also means lag_max = 24 means twenty-four quarters here — six years, not two. How many lags is “enough” always depends on the index unit, which is the subject of slide 25.

    Pattern 1 of 3  ·  trend

    Trend: large and positive at small lags, decaying slowly.

    Read the positive, slowly decreasing bars at the left. Where the ACF drifts below zero is the same decay continuing, and it tells you nothing extra.

    A negative trend gives the identical picture. The coefficients stay positive, because adjacent observations still resemble each other; which way the series is heading does not enter into it. If you expect a downward trend to produce negative bars, you are confusing the ACF with a slope.

    The extreme case is a random walk, where the ACF stays near 1 for a very long stretch and decays almost imperceptibly. That near-flat, near-one correlogram is the classic fingerprint of a series with no stable mean, and it is the first thing an ARIMA course teaches you to look for.

    Pattern 2 of 3  ·  seasonality  ·  recent_beer, the real output

    Seasonality: a spike every m lags, and a trough halfway between.

    Both the spikes and the troughs shrink as you move right — 0.80, 0.71, 0.63, 0.50. That is the distance penalty, not fading seasonality.

    These are the numbers ACF() actually prints for recent_beer, so you can check every bar against your own console.

    The sign pattern generalizes: for a season of length m, expect positive spikes at multiples of m and negative ones near odd multiples of m/2. Monthly data with m = 12 therefore dips near lags 6, 18 and 30. If the negative dips are missing and everything sits above zero, a trend is holding the whole correlogram up — which is the case on the next two slides.

    Pattern 3 of 3  ·  both, of comparable strength

    Both present: a wave riding on a slope.

    Read it in two passes. Envelope first, then ripple. The order matters, because the envelope is what hides things.

    Compare this with slide 15 and you can see exactly what the trend did: on the pure seasonal series the half-period lags went deeply negative, and here the same lags only sag. The trend has lifted the whole picture, and the seasonal signal survives as a wobble rather than an alternation.

    The shapes on this slide and the next two were generated with a small web app the notebook links to, where you can set the relative strength of trend and season and watch the correlogram change: tel5cn-juan-garbayo.shinyapps.io/shiny_tseriesacf. Ten minutes with it is worth more than re-reading this slide.

    04_C  ·  Australian antidiabetic drug sales (a10)

    Same series, two tools, two different answers.

    A loud instrument does not delete a quiet one, it masks it. The seasonality is still in the data; it is missing from this summary of the data.

    Precision matters in how you state this. It is not that the ACF shows nothing seasonal — the notebook's phrasing is that the waves can be seen but “the ACF alone does not reveal the actual seasonal period”, because lag 12 is not clearly higher than its neighbors. What is lost is the period, not the existence of a pattern.

    Push the trend harder still and even the ripple disappears, leaving a correlogram indistinguishable from a pure trend. That is the fourth row of the table on the next slide, and Session 5 is the fix: strip the trend out first, then run the ACF on what is left.

    What to expect, and what it hides

    The ACF reports what dominates, not what exists.

    Trend onlyPositive bars, slow smooth decay. No ripple.Reliable reading.
    Season onlySpikes at multiples of m, deep troughs at half-periods, near-zero elsewhere.Reliable reading. Gives you m.
    ComparableA decaying envelope with a ripple on it. Both readable.Read envelope, then ripple.
    One dominatesOnly the dominant one shows. Strong trend → looks like pure trend; strong season → the trend vanishes.Needs decomposition.

    Two of these four rows will mislead you if the correlogram is the only thing you look at. That is an argument for always plotting the series first, not against the ACF.

    The last row runs in both directions, and the trend-hidden-by-season case gets less attention than it deserves. A strongly seasonal series with a mild drift produces a correlogram whose spikes are so dominant that the gentle background decay is invisible — and people then report “seasonal, no trend” from a series that is clearly drifting on its time plot.

    Both failure modes have the same resolution, and it is the whole of Session 5: decompose the series, then look at the components separately. An ACF of the remainder after decomposition is a much sharper instrument than an ACF of the raw series, and it is how residual diagnostics work in Session 9.

    The habit to build

    Four questions, in this order, every correlogram.

    1 · Envelope

    Do the bars start high and decay slowly? That is trend.

    2 · Rhythm

    Are there peaks at regular intervals? The spacing is m.

    3 · Sign

    Deep negatives at half-periods confirm a season with no trend holding it up.

    4 · Scale

    Is anything big enough to matter? Compare against ±2/√T.

    And one rule above all four: when the correlogram disagrees with the time plot, the time plot wins. A summary can hide a component; a picture of the data cannot.

    These deliberately echo Session 3's four questions about a time plot, and they are meant to be used together rather than in competition. Question 4 is the one the rest of this session is about.

    Checking every bar against the bounds by eye is a slightly disreputable thing to do, statistically, and Session 9 replaces it with a single test over many lags at once — the Ljung–Box portmanteau test. Until then the visual rule is what you have, and the next three slides are about using it honestly.

    The reference case

    White noise is a series with no memory at all.

    • No autocorrelation at any lag. That is the definition — not “looks messy”, but knowing yt−k tells you nothing about yt, for every k.
    • The notebook's example draws from a normal distribution, which makes it Gaussian white noise specifically. set.seed(30) fixes the draw so everyone's plot matches.
    • Its ACF will not be exactly zero. Fifty random numbers will produce some accidental resemblance at some lag. The question is how much is too much.

    White noise is the null hypothesis of this whole course. Every model you build from Session 8 onward is a claim that you can do better than it.

    Two distinctions worth keeping straight, both of which come back later.

    First, white noise is not the same as Gaussian. The definition requires zero autocorrelation and a constant mean and variance; the normal distribution is an extra assumption, convenient and often reasonable, and it is what rnorm() supplies here. Second, and more subtly, uncorrelated is not the same as independent — which is exactly the point Session 3 made with the temperature-and-demand U-curve, where r was 0.26 and the relationship was enormous. Session 2's stochastic-process framing is where this bites: the IID assumption behind the CLT is stronger than anything the ACF can check.

    Now we compute

    Fifty draws, and their correlogram

    set.seed(30) y <- tsibble(sample = 1:50, wn = rnorm(50), index = sample) y |> autoplot(wn) y |> ACF(wn) |> autoplot()
    T = 50, so the bounds sit at ±2/√50 = ±0.283. They are drawn for you as dashed blue lines. Count how many of your spikes cross them before you turn the page.
    set.seed() makes a random draw reproducible: it fixes the starting state of R's pseudo-random generator, so rnorm(50) returns the same fifty numbers on every machine. Remove the seed and you get a different series — and a different correlogram — every time you run the chunk. Both are legitimate; only one is teachable.

    Running it a few times without the seed is genuinely instructive. You will see spikes drift in and out of the bounds at random lags, which is the clearest possible demonstration that a single crossing means very little.

    Telling noise from signal

    For white noise, 95% of spikes fall inside ±2/√T.

    The bounds narrow as the series gets longer: ±0.28 at T = 50, ±0.06 at T = 1000. A long series lets you detect a much weaker memory.

    The band is an illustration of the shape, not a reproduction of the set.seed(30) output — check your own console for that, and count the crossings yourself.

    Where the 2 comes from: the exact 95% multiplier for a normal distribution is 1.96, and 2 is the rounding everyone uses. So ±2/√T is very slightly wider than a true 95% band, which is the conservative direction to err in. And note what the shrinking band implies about long series — with T large enough, a trivially small autocorrelation becomes “significant”. Statistical significance and practical importance part company here, as they do everywhere.

    The decision rule

    When should you say a series is not white noise?

    • A spike is clearly outside, not grazing the line. Size is evidence; borderline is not.
    • Substantially more than 5% of spikes are outside. One in twenty is the expected rate. Five in twenty is a finding; two is not.
    • There is structure in the heights. A decay, or spikes at regular intervals. Noise has no shape — a pattern is worth more than any single tall bar.

    Any one of the three is enough. The third is the strongest, because it is the one chance cannot easily fake.

    The notebook gives the first two criteria; the third is implicit in every example it shows, and it is the one that will serve you best in the exam and afterwards.

    Note the asymmetry in what this rule can do. Crossing the bounds is evidence against white noise. Staying inside them is not proof of white noise — it is a failure to find evidence, on a test that is weak for short series. In Session 9 this matters a great deal, because the thing you will be testing for white noise is a set of model residuals, and “I could not detect any remaining structure” is a more honest claim than “there is none”.

    Before the homework  ·  04_D

    A lag is one step of whatever your index happens to be.

    • So lag_max is not a style choice. A yearly season cannot appear in a correlogram that stops short of one full cycle in the units you are indexed by.
    • Which makes aggregation part of the analysis. index_by() plus summarise() changes the unit, and with it the lag you should be looking at — and T, and therefore the bounds.

    That is all 04_D is: choosing the resolution at which the question can be asked. Do it before you reach for the ACF, not after.

    04_D itself contains no ACF work — it is pure aggregation and time plots, taking hourly Beijing pollution up to weekly and monthly, and weekly fuel prices up to quarterly. Treat it as the setup step that the correlogram depends on.

    One detail in the fuel-prices exercise repays attention: the notebook asks you to try index_by() with and without a preceding group_by(fuel_type). Without it, petrol and diesel are averaged together into a single meaningless series. That is the same keyed-tsibble idea from Session 2, and it is the sort of silent wrong answer that no warning will catch for you.

    04_C  ·  exercise 1 — start it here

    Four series. Four correlograms. Work out the pairing.

    • Look at the four correlograms first, before the series. Classify each on its own: envelope, rhythm, sign, scale.
    • Then look at the four time plots and do the same: trend, seasonality, cycles, damage.
    • Match on the strongest feature. One clean discriminating feature is worth more than a general impression of similarity.
    Check the sampling interval of every series before you match anything. A spike at lag 12 means one thing in monthly data and something quite different in quarterly or annual data — and annual data, as Session 3 insisted, cannot be seasonal at all.
    Classifying each picture on its own before attempting any pairing is the whole method, and it is worth the discipline — matching by overall vibe is how people talk themselves into a wrong pair and then defend it.

    A useful sanity check when you finish: every correlogram should be claimed exactly once. If two of your series want the same correlogram, the feature you matched on is not discriminating, and you should go back and find one that is.
