    Why this course exists

    Every plan is a forecast.

    • “We hire 40 people next quarter.” Somebody forecast demand.
    • “Order 10,000 units.” Somebody forecast sales.
    • “We will close the year at €12M.” Somebody forecast revenue.

    The forecast is already inside the decision. The only question is whether anyone checked it.

    Ask for a show of hands: who has ever seen a number in a plan and wondered where it came from. Do not take answers yet, just plant it. Keep this slide to about ninety seconds.

    Both errors cost money.

    You cannot opt out of forecasting. You only choose how badly you do it.

    The right-hand error is the expensive one and almost nobody measures it, because a sale that never happened leaves no record. Worth saying out loud: the stockout is invisible in your data.

    The one idea of this course

    A number is a guess.
    A distribution is a forecast.

    Only one of those can be planned against. You will see it on my screen in about forty minutes.

    Do not explain intervals yet. This slide only has to make the shape memorable, so that the N(47, 0.1) moment later in the live run lands as a payoff rather than as new material.

    Yesterday tells you something about today.

    That single fact breaks most of the toolkit you already have. Rebuilding it is this course. Session 2 starts exactly here.

    session01.R  ·  section 1

    How to read a tsibble.

    # A tsibble: 15,150 x 9 [1Y] # Key: Country [263]

    [1Y]One observation per year. That is the interval.

    Country [263]263 separate series, stacked in one table.

    The index is the time column. The key is what tells the series apart. A plain data frame has neither.

    Get a student to read the header out loud. Then filter to Spain and have them notice the key change to Country [1] and the row count drop to 58. The header is the teaching object here, not the data.

    Look at the fable. Two columns, side by side.

    N(211, 0.0021)

    That is not a number. It is a normal distribution. Brazil, 2018, in millions of people.

    Pop  <dist>The forecast. A whole distribution, mean and variance.

    .mean  <dbl>211. The number everybody quotes. Only a summary of the column to its left.

    A forecast is a random variable.

    setup/SETUP.md

    The order matters.

    • R first, from cran.r-project.org. Windows also needs Rtools matching your R version. Mac: arm64 for Apple Silicon, x86_64 for Intel.
    • RStudio Desktop second, from posit.co. It is only a front end. It looks for an R that already exists.
    • Packages third, in the RStudio console. Both lines, not just the first.
    install.packages("fpp3") install.packages(c( "tidyverse", "nycflights13", "babynames", "urca", "GGally", "fma", "patchwork", "cowplot", "seasonal" ))
    The second line is not optional. None of those nine come with fpp3. tidyverse is needed for your first homework, and urca is needed by ARIMA() today.

    On Linux, install the development headers listed in setup/SETUP.md before any of this. Quarto needs no separate install: RStudio already ships it.

    The second line is the one that costs an hour if it is missed, because the failure looks nothing like a missing package. R stops and shows a Selection: prompt, then reads the rest of the script as answers to it, and it looks like the script has gone insane.
