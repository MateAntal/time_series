    Where we are  ·  part 2 of 3

    You used trend all Monday. Nobody said where it came from.

    • You subtracted it and divided by it to build detrended and seasonally adjusted series. The column was handed to you.
    • You saw its edges were missing, at both ends of every plot, and were told twice to wait for today.
    • Today is one idea. Slide a window along the series, average what is inside it, and call the result the trend.

    Everything else in this session, including the awkward arithmetic in the middle, is a consequence of that one move.

    Most decomposition algorithms start with the trend, and the reason is practical rather than deep: the trend is easier to pull out of a raw series than the seasonal component is. Once you have the trend you can take it out, and what is left is a much easier problem. That is the order Session 7 follows.

    Moving averages are used well beyond decomposition, which is why the notebook covers a little more than the algorithm strictly needs. Anywhere you have seen a rolling seven-day average of case counts or a fifty-day average of a share price, this is the same object.

    The whole method, before any notation

    Put a window over the series. Average what is inside. Move it one step. Repeat.

    The jagged line is the data. The smooth line underneath is what a sliding average leaves behind. That smooth line is the trend estimate.

    Notation, so that the rest of the session is short. A moving average over m points is written m-MA, so the window drawn here gives the 7-MA of the series.

    The window drawn here is centered: it leaves three points to the left of the point being averaged and three to the right. That is not the only choice, and slide 15 shows the alternatives, but it is the one this course uses and the one classical decomposition is built on.

    Both of which you have already seen

    Neighboring windows differ in exactly two points.

    Both of Monday's loose ends, answered by the same picture. Keep the diagram: the second half of this session is nothing but it with the window widths changed.

    The count generalizes: an m-MA loses (m-1)/2 values at each end, so a 13-MA gives up six at each end and a 2×12-MA gives up six as well. The script prints these counts for every window it builds, and it is worth checking the arithmetic against the output once.

    Missing values at the ends are not a defect to be fixed. They are an honest statement that the estimate needs neighbours on both sides and does not have them there. Slide 16 comes back to what that costs you, because it is the most consequential limitation in this block.

    Notation for a centered window

    m has to be odd, so that the window has a middle.

    m-MA_t = (1/m) * sum of y_{t+j} for j = -k .. +k m = 2k + 1 # the 7-MA, written out 7-MA_t = (1/7)(y_{t-3} + y_{t-2} + y_{t-1} + y_t + y_{t+1} + y_{t+2} + y_{t+3})
    • k points on each side, plus the point itself. That is where 2k + 1 comes from, and why it can never be even.
    • In R this is slider::slide_dbl(), with .before = k and .after = k. The argument names are the notation.
    • .complete = TRUE is the right setting. It computes no average on a partial window and returns NA instead.

    Hold on to the word odd. In ten minutes it becomes a problem, because the seasonal periods we care about are twelve and four.

    Do not skip past .complete = TRUE. Set it to FALSE and R averages whatever part of the window exists, so the first value of a 7-MA would be the mean of four points rather than seven. You get a series with no gaps and a quietly different definition at each end, which is worse than a gap because nothing tells you it happened.

    The word for an odd-numbered window in some other languages translates as “uneven”, and you will see that word in older course material. In English uneven means not level. The word here is odd.

    Average seven numbers yourself. Ask R whether it agrees.

    mean(aus_exports$Exports[1:7]) aus_exports$`7-MA`[[4]] all.equal(mean(aus_exports$Exports[1:7]), aus_exports$`7-MA`[[4]]) isTRUE(all.equal(mean(aus_exports$Exports[1:7]), aus_exports$`7-MA`[[4]]))
    • Why element 4 and not element 1. The first three windows are incomplete, so the first number a 7-MA can produce sits in row four.
    • This is Monday's idiom, on its first real job. all.equal() alone returns TRUE or a sentence; isTRUE() turns it into something you can act on.
    • Do this at every step in Session 7. Four steps, four checks. A decomposition that fails at step 3 is much cheaper to find at step 3.

    Work out which element to compare before you run it. Getting the index right is most of the exercise, and it is the same reasoning that tells you how many values are missing.

    If you do reach for == here it will probably return TRUE, because both sides happen to be computed by very similar arithmetic. That is luck, not correctness, and it will run out in Session 7 where your route to a number and R's differ by several steps.

    A second check worth running yourself: compare mean(Exports[2:8]) against element 5, and satisfy yourself that the window really has moved by one and not by seven. It is a thirty-second experiment that settles the difference between a moving average and a block average for good.

    06_B  ·  3-MA through 13-MA  ·  on the RStudio screen

    A wider window buys smoothness and pays for it at the ends.

    There is no free smoothing. Every point you add to the window is a point you lose from each end of the answer.

    The script prints colSums(is.na(...)) across all six moving averages so you can read the cost straight off: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 missing values in total, which is (m-1)/2 at each end. Check one of them by hand against the rule.

    This is a genuine trade-off rather than a setting with a right answer, and it is worth being suspicious of anyone who smooths a series without saying how much. A long enough moving average will make any series look like it has a clean trend, including one that is pure noise. Session 4's correlogram is the check on whether there was anything there to smooth.

    The reason this session is not just about smoothing

    Average over exactly one seasonal period and the season cancels itself out.

    • One full seasonal period contains every phase once. Every high month and every low month, exactly once each, so they offset.
    • So the width is not a free choice. Pick m equal to the seasonal period and the moving average returns the trend with the season removed.

    This is why Session 7 can estimate the trend first and the season second. The window does the separating.

    The cancellation is exact only if the seasonal pattern repeats identically from one period to the next, for monthly data from year to year, which is the assumption classical decomposition makes and where it is weakest. Session 7 names that limitation and STL is the method that relaxes it.

    This also explains a detail from Session 5 that may have looked arbitrary. The trend column from classical_decomposition() on monthly data was missing six values at each end, not five or seven, because the window it used spans thirteen points. The next two slides are where that thirteen comes from.

    The problem this session exists to solve

    The seasonal periods that matter are even. Even windows have no middle.

    • Weekly data is fine. A seven-day seasonal period gives an odd window, three days each side, and everything above applies unchanged.
    • Quarterly and monthly are not. Four and twelve are even, so any window of that width has one more point on one side than the other.

    Two requirements that will not both hold: the window must span one seasonal period, and the window must be centered. The next slide keeps both.

    You could simply accept the imbalance and use a window with one extra point on the right. The result is a trend estimate shifted half a step in time, and half a step of bias in every value of a component you are about to subtract from the data. Small, systematic, and avoidable, which is the worst kind of error to leave in.

    Note which cases are affected. Daily data with weekly seasonality is odd and needs nothing. Monthly, quarterly, and half-hourly data with daily seasonality are all even. In practice the even case is the common one, which is why this is a section of the course rather than a footnote.

    The fix, in three steps

    Lean left. Lean right. Average the two, and you are centered again.

    • Compute an m-MA leaning one way: one point to the left, two to the right for m = 4. Call it 4-MA R.
    • Compute an m-MA leaning the other way: two to the left, one to the right. Call it 4-MA L.
    • Average those two series. One leans forward by half a step, the other leans back by half a step, and the two errors cancel exactly.
    Written 2 × m-MA. The 2 counts how many moving averages were averaged, not how many points are in the window. 2×4-MA and 2×12-MA are the two you will use.

    Nothing subtle is happening here. It is the same trick as taking the mean of two estimates that are wrong in opposite directions.

    The naming trips people up every year, so read it carefully: in 2×4-MA the 4 is the window width and the 2 is the number of passes. It is not a window of eight points. The next slide shows that it actually spans five.

    The same construction generalizes: a p×m-MA means take an m-MA and then a p-MA of that, and the result spans p + m - 1 points. Exercises 3 and 4 are two instances of exactly this, which is why they can be done on paper.

    The algebra, done once

    Average two four-point windows and you get a five-point weighted one.

    4-MA_t R = (1/4)[ y_{t-1} + y_t + y_{t+1} + y_{t+2} ] 4-MA_t L = (1/4)[ y_{t-2} + y_{t-1} + y_t + y_{t+1} ] (1/2)[ R + L ] = (1/8)y_{t-2} + (1/4)y_{t-1} + (1/4)y_t + (1/4)y_{t+1} + (1/8)y_{t+2}
    • Symmetric, and they sum to 1. Both properties are what make this an average at all rather than an arbitrary combination.
    • The general rule: a 2×m-MA spans m+1 points, every weight 1/m, except the two ends at 1/(2m).

    The ends carry half weight because each of them appeared in only one of the two windows. Nothing was chosen; it fell out of the arithmetic.

    For monthly data the same rule gives a 2×12-MA spanning thirteen points, with weights of 1/12 in the middle and 1/24 at each end. Thirteen points, six on each side, which is exactly the six missing values you counted at each end of Session 5's trend column.

    Half weight at the ends makes sense for monthly data. January appears twice in a thirteen-month window centered on July, once at each end, and giving each appearance half weight counts it exactly once overall. The season is still cancelled, and the window is still centered.

    Now we compute  ·  quarterly beer, m = 4

    Two imbalanced windows, then their mean

    `4-MA_R` = slide_dbl(Beer, mean, .before = 1, .after = 2, .complete = TRUE) `4-MA_L` = slide_dbl(Beer, mean, .before = 2, .after = 1, .complete = TRUE) `2x4-MA` = 0.5 * (`4-MA_R` + `4-MA_L`)
    • Read head() and tail() before the plot. Watch where each of the three columns starts and stops, and check the gaps against the rule.
    • The two one-sided columns die at different ends. Their average is defined only where both are, which is what makes the final gap symmetric.

    Quarterly beer is the same series Session 4 used for the correlogram. Same data, third tool, and the seasonal period is still four.

    Work through the .before and .after numbers rather than memorizing them. For 4-MA R we want four points with one extra on the right, so one before and two after, plus the point itself: 1 + 1 + 2 = 4. For 4-MA L, two before and one after: 2 + 1 + 1 = 4. The arithmetic is trivial and the slip is common.

    Anyone confident with dplyr may notice that classical_decomposition() and STL do all of this internally and you will never write these two lines in anger. That is true, and beside the point. Session 7 asks you to build the whole algorithm, and this is the step that makes the others possible.

    06_B  ·  the check worth running

    Build it from the weights instead, and confirm the two agree.

    `2x4-MA_weights` = (1/8) * lag(Beer, 2) + (1/4) * lag(Beer, 1) + (1/4) * Beer + (1/4) * lead(Beer, 1) + (1/8) * lead(Beer, 2) isTRUE(all.equal(beer$`2x4-MA`, beer$`2x4-MA_weights`))
    • This is the slide 12 algebra, run as code. Two independent routes to the same column, and no reason beyond the derivation to expect them to match.
    • lag() and lead() are Session 4's tools. lag(Beer, 2) is y_{t-2}, and if you find the direction confusing you are in good company.
    • It returns TRUE. That is the point: derivations you have checked are derivations you can use in an exam without anxiety.

    This is the whole method of the block in miniature. Derive on paper, implement twice, compare. Session 7 does it four times over.

    Note that this only works at all because of .complete = TRUE. The two-step route produces NA wherever a window was incomplete; the weighted route produces NA wherever lag() or lead() ran off the end of the column. Those are the same places, which is itself a small confirmation that the two constructions describe the same object.

    If you want to see the check fail, change one weight from 1/8 to 1/7 and run it again. You will get a character string back, describing a mean relative difference somewhere around a thousandth. Seeing the failure mode once is worth more than being told about it.

    The alternative we are not using

    A one-sided window reaches the end of the series. It pays with a lag.

    # left: the point and the m-1 points before it left m-MA_t = (1/m) * sum of y_{t+j} for j = -(m-1) .. 0 # right: the point and the m-1 points after it right m-MA_t = (1/m) * sum of y_{t+j} for j = 0 .. (m-1)
    • The left version can be computed at the final observation, because everything it needs is already in the past. That is why trading and monitoring dashboards use it.
    • It sits behind the data. Its value at time t describes a window whose center was half a window ago, so it turns late at every turning point.
    • This course uses centered windows only. Classical decomposition needs the season cancelled without a time shift, and only a centered window does that.

    Run the two lines in the script once and watch the orange line lag and the blue line lead. Nothing on this slide makes the case as well.

    The fifty-day moving average quoted on financial charts is a left moving average, and the lag is not a flaw in that setting: the whole point is that it uses no information from the future, because in trading there is none to be had. Different job, different window.

    Some implementations shift gradually to a one-sided window as they approach the ends, so that the trend estimate reaches the last observation. That buys you a number where you had a gap, at the cost of a value computed by a different rule from all the others. classical_decomposition() does not do this, and leaves the gaps instead.

    The limitation that matters most

    The trend value you most want is the one you cannot have.

    • Both gaps are the same size. Only one of them hurts. Nobody needs the trend in 1990; everybody wants it for last month.
    • This is why commentary on recent data goes wrong. The seasonally adjusted series does reach today, but it carries the remainder, which is Session 5's slide 14.

    Decomposition is a tool for understanding what a series has done, not for calling what it is doing right now. Forecasting, from Session 8, is the tool for the second question.

    The same shape recurs throughout the course. Anything estimated from a symmetric window of neighbours is weakest where the neighbours run out, and the end of a series is always where the decisions are being made. It is worth stating out loud when you present any decomposition to somebody who will act on it.

    This is also the answer to a question that comes up in Group Assignment 1: no, you cannot fix it by using a shorter window at the ends, or at least not without changing what the number means. What you can do is say clearly that the last few points carry no trend estimate, and let the reader draw their own conclusion about the most recent months.
