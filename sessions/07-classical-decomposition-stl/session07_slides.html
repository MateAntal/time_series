    Classical decomposition, additive

    Trend first, because it is the only one you can get without the others.

    One of the four steps is new. The order is not negotiable: each step consumes the output of the one above it.

    Why the trend has to come first: the seasonal component is estimated by averaging, and averaging only isolates the season once the trend is out of the way. Leave the trend in and every seasonal average is contaminated by wherever the series happened to be at the time.

    The multiplicative version is the same four boxes with divisions in place of subtractions, plus one difference in step 3 that slide 8 covers. If you can state the additive version cleanly you can derive the multiplicative one in the exam without memorizing it separately.

    The seasonal component, in three moves

    Average every January together. Then every February. Then repeat.

    Twelve numbers describe the whole season, and then they repeat unchanged forever. That sentence is both the method and its biggest limitation.

    In R the repeating step is a left_join() on the month of the year. The twelve-row table joins onto the full series and every row collects the seasonal value for its own month. Nothing clever, and it is where an off-by-one in the month column would show up immediately.

    Note what the averaging assumes. Every January in the sample contributes equally to S_1, whether it came from 1990 or 2019. If January's behaviour has drifted over thirty years, this method cannot see it and will report the average of the two eras, which matches neither. STL, at the end of today, is the answer to that.

    Step 3.2  ·  the bit that looks arbitrary and is not

    Those twelve numbers will not sum to zero. Make them.

    S_i = S_unadj_i - mean(S_unadj) # additive: sum to 0 S_i = S_unadj_i / mean(S_unadj) # multiplicative: average to 1
    • Why they need it. Nothing in step 3.1 forced the twelve averages to be centered. Left alone, they carry a bit of level that belongs to the trend.
    • What the condition buys. With the season summing to zero, the season contributes nothing to the average of the series over one full period. The level stays entirely in the trend.
    • Why the multiplicative version divides. Centering on 1 is to multiplication what centering on 0 is to addition. A factor of 1 changes nothing, exactly as adding 0 changes nothing.

    Both corrections are one line, and the notebook derives both. Do the derivation once: it is the cleanest small piece of algebra in the course.

    Subtracting the mean from any vector makes it sum to zero, and dividing any vector by its mean makes it average to 1. Neither fact is specific to seasonality, which is why the notebook proves each in three lines and then applies it.

    The sanity check is worth building into your code rather than doing once by eye. After step 3.2, isTRUE(all.equal(sum(seasonal), 0)) for an additive fit, or isTRUE(all.equal(mean(seasonal), 1)) for a multiplicative one. If it fails, the error is in step 3, and you have saved yourself from debugging step 4.

    Now we compute  ·  07_D

    Four steps, four checks

    • Fit classical_decomposition() first, and keep its output as the reference
    • Step 1 — the 2×12-MA, then isTRUE(all.equal(trend_manual, reference$trend))
    • Step 2 — Employed - trend_manual, and check
    • Step 3 — average by month, center on zero, left_join(), and check
    • Step 4 — detrended - seasonal, and check against random
    Check as you go, not at the end. A decomposition that fails at step 3 is far cheaper to find at step 3 than in the plot at the end.
    Two column-name traps, both of which will bite somebody today. classical_decomposition() calls the remainder random, not remainder. And it exposes no detrended column at all, so step 2's check compares against Employed - trend computed from the reference.

    All four checks return TRUE on this series, and that is not a coincidence you should take for granted. It means the algorithm in the notebook is the algorithm the package implements, step for step, and it is the strongest evidence you will get that you have understood it.

    Two criteria, both about the leftovers

    Judge a decomposition by what it failed to explain.

    • 1. The remainder's variance should be small — smaller than the trend's and the season's. If the leftovers move more than the components, the components are not doing the work.
    • 2. The remainder should have as little autocorrelation as possible. Structure left in the remainder is structure the model missed. The closer to white noise, the better.
    • Sometimes neither can be met. Then take the decomposition that comes closest, and say out loud where it falls short.
    Criterion 2 is Session 4, reused. Run ACF() on the remainder and read it exactly as you read any other correlogram. Session 9 does the same thing to model residuals.

    Notice what is not on this list: whether the trend line looks nice. That judgement is worth very little, and it is the one everybody reaches for first.

    The grey bars down the left of an autoplot() panel are how you read criterion 1 without computing anything. Each bar covers the same range in data units, so a tall bar marks a panel whose range is small. A remainder panel with a tall bar is good news; one with a bar as short as the seasonal panel's is a warning.

    Criterion 2 has a formal version, the Ljung–Box test, which replaces eyeballing twenty bars with a single number. It arrives in Session 9, where the thing being tested is a set of forecast residuals rather than a decomposition remainder. The logic is identical.

    Where classical decomposition gives up

    Three limitations, and you have already met the first two.

    The endsNo trend, and therefore no remainder, for the first and last (m − 1)/2 points.Session 6. Not fixable without changing what the number means.
    Fixed seasonThe same m values repeat from the first year to the last. A season that evolves cannot be represented.STL, later today.
    OutliersOne unusual value contaminates the trend, then the detrended series, then the seasonal average.Exercise 1 makes you watch it happen.
    • The chain runs in one direction. The outlier reaches the seasonal component only by passing through the trend and the detrended series first.
    • Cut the chain at the first link and nothing propagates. That is what the missing trend values at the ends do.

    The outlier case has a twist worth predicting before you run it: where in the series the outlier sits changes the answer completely.

    The twist, in case you want to reason it out rather than discover it: the trend is a 2×4-MA, and Session 6 established that a moving average cannot be computed near the ends of a series. An outlier in the middle lands in the region the moving average can reach, so it is absorbed into the trend, passed into the detrended series, and then averaged into the seasonal component. An outlier at the very end lands where no trend value exists, so it never enters the chain at all.

    That is a strange kind of robustness. The decomposition is undamaged because it had already given up on that part of the series.

    The other algorithm  ·  fpp3 3.6

    Seasonal and Trend decomposition using LOESS.

    • LOESS is local regression. To fit a value at a point, take its nearest neighbors, weight them by closeness, and fit a straight or slightly curved line to just those.
    • You never choose a global formula. No straight line, no polynomial for the whole series. Only a shape for the local pieces, and how big a neighborhood to use.

    STL applies that idea repeatedly to pull the components apart. The size of the neighborhood is the one thing you control, and it is the whole of the tuning.

    Classical decomposition's trend is a moving average, which is a local mean. LOESS fits a local line instead. That one change is why STL follows a bend better than a moving average of comparable width, and why it can produce a trend estimate closer to the ends.

    The full algorithm iterates between estimating the season and estimating the trend, refining each with the other held fixed, and it is genuinely more involved than anything you will be asked to reproduce. You are expected to know what the windows do and how to judge the result, not to implement it.

    Both sides of the trade

    It fixes the fixed season. It cannot do multiplication.

    Buys you

    A season that may change over time, at a rate you set. Any seasonal period, several at once. A trend whose smoothness you control. Optional robustness to outliers.

    Costs you

    Additive only. No automatic calendar or trading-day handling. Windows that usually need tuning by hand rather than sensible defaults you can trust.

    • The additive-only limit is not a real obstacle, because Session 5 already solved it: take logs, decompose, and the multiplication has become addition.
    • The tuning is the real cost. Two windows, both odd, both changed by trial, and judged by the two criteria from slide 7 rather than by eye.

    Classical decomposition has no knobs and one answer. STL has two knobs and a judgement to make. That is the whole difference in practice.

    A caution about robustness that the notebook makes and is easy to misread: robust = TRUE keeps unusual observations from distorting the trend and the season, but they still land in the remainder, where they belong. It also does not always improve a decomposition, so it is worth trying both ways rather than switching it on by reflex.

    “Several seasonal periods at once” is the one advantage you cannot appreciate yet. Half-hourly electricity demand has a daily cycle, a weekly cycle and a yearly cycle running simultaneously, and classical decomposition can represent exactly one of them. That is the Session 3 electricity example returning with a method that can handle it.

    The only two settings you will touch

    Smaller window, faster change. Both numbers must be odd.

    STL(Employed ~ trend(window = 21) + season(window = 13)) default default
    trend(window)How fast the trend-cycle may change. How many consecutive observations feed each local fit.Smaller = follows bends. Default 21.
    season(window)How fast the seasonal shape may change. How many consecutive seasons feed each seasonal value.Smaller = season evolves. Default 13.
    "periodic"An infinite seasonal window: every seasonal value uses the whole series, so the season comes out constant.This is classical decomposition's assumption.
    • Start from the defaults and change one at a time. Moving both at once tells you nothing about which one helped.
    • A window that is too small is a real failure, not just a cautious setting. Narrow the trend enough and it starts swallowing the seasonal wiggles.

    Notice the last row. Setting season(window = "periodic") throws away the one thing STL had over the algorithm you built this morning.

    Read the units carefully, because they differ between the two windows. The trend window counts observations. The seasonal window counts seasons, so season(window = 13) on monthly data means each seasonal value is estimated from thirteen Januaries, not thirteen months.

    The default of 13 is a deliberate middle: wide enough that the seasonal estimate is not chasing noise, narrow enough that a genuinely drifting season can move. Both windows must be odd for the same reason a moving average window must be odd, which is that a local fit needs a center.

    Now we compute  ·  07_E

    Same series as this morning. Watch where 2008 ends up.

    • Run STL with the defaults on us_retail_employment and look at the remainder panel around 2008. The crisis is sitting in it.
    • That is a diagnosis, not an opinion. A recession is not random noise, so a remainder holding one is a remainder holding structure — criterion 2, failed.
    • Narrow the trend window and run it again. The trend can now bend into the crisis, and the remainder gets quieter.
    Judge every change with the ACF of the remainder, not with how the trend line looks. The picture improves long before the diagnostic does.
    This is the same 2008 dip that Session 5 pointed out as a cycle, which is why it belongs in the trend-cycle component rather than the remainder. STL with default windows is too stiff to take it, so it falls through. Narrowing the trend window is how you let the trend-cycle component do the job its name promises.

    There is a limit to how far this goes, and it is worth reaching it once. Keep narrowing the trend window and eventually the trend starts following the seasonal wiggles too, which empties the seasonal component into the trend. Both extremes are visible in a few seconds of experimenting, and the useful setting is somewhere between them.

    07_E  ·  the outcome to report

    Sometimes there is no setting that works, and saying so is the answer.

    • The labour series has a crisis in 1991 to 1992 that no pair of windows fully absorbs. Narrow them and the remainder improves a little, then stops.
    • The daily electricity series is worse. Its seasonal pattern genuinely differs at the start and end of the year, and the remainder's ACF stays far from white noise whatever you do.
    • The correct report is not a better number. It is: here is the closest I got, here is where it fails, and here is what I would need instead.

    A decomposition that does not work, honestly described, is a finding. A decomposition that does not work, presented as though it does, is the thing this course is trying to prevent.

    What you would need instead, for the curious: an event like a recession can be handled by telling the model explicitly that it happened, with an intervention variable, rather than hoping a smoother absorbs it. That belongs to regression with time series predictors, which is in the sequel course rather than this one.

    Comparing two remainders by eye across twenty ACF bars is unsatisfying, and you should feel that. The Ljung–Box statistic in Session 9 collapses the comparison into one number per decomposition, and it is the right tool for exactly this question.

    The practical question

    Which one should you actually use?

    Classical

    When you need to explain every step, when the season is genuinely stable, and when you are being marked on understanding rather than on fit. It is transparent and it has no settings to defend.

    STL

    Almost everywhere else. When the season drifts, when there are outliers, when the period is unusual, or when there is more than one period at once.

    • In short: classical decomposition is what you build to understand decomposition. STL is what you run when you need an answer.
    • Both are judged the same way, by the two criteria on slide 7. The criteria do not care which algorithm produced the remainder.

    Group Assignment 1 asks for the first one, built by hand. That is a deliberate choice about what is being assessed.

    Neither of these is a forecasting method, and it is worth being clear about that before Session 8 arrives. A decomposition describes a series you already have. It has no opinion about the next observation, and the components it produces stop where the data stops.

    Decomposition does turn up again inside forecasting, though: one common approach forecasts the seasonally adjusted series and then puts the seasonal component back on top. You meet that once you have a forecasting method to put inside it.
