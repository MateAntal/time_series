    In Session 8 you produced forecasts. Today asks whether the model behind them left anything on the table.

    • A model that runs is not a model that works. Sessions 9 to 14 are about telling the difference: today checks what the model left behind, Sessions 11 and 12 fix the spread and make the ranges honest, and Sessions 13 and 14 measure how large the errors are.
    • This session is not on the midterm. It is examined in the final, which covers the whole course.

    Residuals exist only for fitted values. et = yt - ŷt|t-1: each fitted value sits inside the data, so there is an observation to subtract it from. A forecast has none yet. Everything today reads that one column.

    fpp3 5.4  ·  the 09_A summary sheet in one slide

    A model has done its job when nothing but noise is left.

    Four properties, two questions. Knowing which question a failure belongs to tells you whether to change the model or change what you claim.

    The summary sheet in this folder, 09_A (a web page, with the handwritten PDF original beside it), sets these out with what to do about each failure, and it is the single most useful thing to have beside you during Group Assignment 2. Print it.

    A point about ordering that is easy to miss: properties 1 and 2 are checked to decide whether a model is finished, and properties 3 and 4 to decide what you are entitled to say. A model can pass 1 and 2 and still leave you unable to quote an honest interval, and that is not a failure of the forecast. It is a limit on the claim.

    All four properties are properties of the residuals, not of the series and not of the forecasts. Strictly, fpp3 states them for the innovation residuals, the .innov column; until Session 11 that column is identical to .resid, so "the residuals" is exact for today.

    Property 1  ·  the hard one

    This is the ACF plot, used for the third time.

    • Structure left in the residuals is structure the model failed to use. A forecast that could have been better, and was not.
    • Unlike bias, this is hard to fix. You need a richer model, not an adjustment: autoregressive terms, external regressors, a different family.
    • Read it exactly as in Session 4. Same bounds, ±2/√T, same caution about single crossings.
    Remainder or residual? A remainder is what a description leaves over. A residual is what a prediction got wrong.

    If you understood Session 4, you already know how to read this plot. What is new is what a crossing now implies: not a feature of the data, but a shortcoming of the model.

    Two names, one plot: Session 4 called it the correlogram as well as the ACF plot. This deck says ACF plot, because that is what fpp3 5.4, the 09_B notebook and the gg_tsresiduals() panel all say.

    Run the ACF plot on .innov rather than .resid. With no transformation they are identical, so it makes no difference today, but from Session 11 on the innovation residuals are the ones the model actually worked with, and those are the ones whose structure is meaningful.

    This is also the point where Session 4's white noise stops being an abstraction. The null hypothesis of the whole course was that a series has no memory; the hope for a set of residuals is that they do not either. Same statement, opposite sign of hope.

    The middle panel and the right one look alike but hold different things. A decomposition remainder is what is left of the series once the trend and season are taken out, and the trend came from a centered window that used values after each point, so the remainder describes the series rather than recording an error. Nothing requires it to be white noise. A residual is the gap between each observation and its fitted value, the model's one-step prediction of that point, so it is the model's error, and a good model should leave white noise behind. A fitted value is not always a true forecast, though: naive and seasonal naive use earlier data only, but the mean and drift models estimate their parameters from the whole series, later points included. That is why residuals cannot stand in for forecast errors, and why Session 13 holds data back.

    White noise means something different in each panel. In a remainder, it means the trend and season captured all the structure in the series. In the residuals, it means the model has used everything the series' own past can tell you about its next value. In both cases it means no pattern is left, not that little is left: a large remainder or large residuals can still be white noise, and how large the errors are is Session 13's question.

    Property 2  ·  the easy one

    A non-zero mean means biased forecasts, and it costs one subtraction to fix.

    • What it means. The model is systematically high or low. Every forecast it makes carries the same offset.
    • How to fix it. Subtract the mean of the residuals from the forecasts. That is the whole remedy, and it is why this property is the least worrying of the four.
    • What it does not tell you. A residual mean of zero is not evidence of a good model. The mean model's residuals average to zero by construction, and it can still be a terrible forecast.
    Bias and uselessness are different problems. A biased model that otherwise fits well is nearly fixed. An unbiased model with autocorrelated residuals is not.

    Check it, fix it if it fails, and do not congratulate a model for passing it.

    The reason the mean model passes automatically is one line of algebra. Its fitted value everywhere is the sample mean, so the residuals are the deviations from the mean, and those sum to zero by definition. Exactly the same algebra as centering the seasonal component in Session 7.

    In practice a visibly non-zero residual mean often points at something more interesting than bias: a trend the model has not captured, which shows up as residuals mostly above zero early and mostly below later, or the reverse. Look at the time plot before reaching for the subtraction.

    Property 3  ·  the first of two about the interval, not the forecast

    If the spread drifts, no single interval width is honest.

    • What it means. The residuals spread about as widely at the end of the series as at the start (homoscedasticity). A prediction interval uses one width, estimated from the whole history, so it is only honest if that holds.
    • What a failure does. If the spread grows, that one width is too wide at the start and too narrow at the end, which is exactly where the forecast begins.
    • How to fix it. The usual cause is a multiplicative series fitted additively, so a log transformation is the first thing to try. Session 11, Box–Cox transformations, turns that into a principled choice.

    A drifting spread does not move the point forecast. It makes the range you quote around it too narrow where it matters most.

    Non-constant variance in a residual plot usually looks like a funnel: the spread widening left to right. It very often means the series is multiplicative and was fitted additively, which is Session 5's question arriving with a consequence. A log transformation is the first thing to try.

    The residual time plot shows the spread only partly. Later today, on the QQ plots and boxplots slide, boxplots of the residuals by year make a drifting spread much easier to see.

    Property 4  ·  the second about the interval, and the forgiving one

    Normality is what turns a standard deviation into a 95% range.

    • Why it matters. A 95% prediction interval is the forecast ± 1.96 × the residual standard deviation. The 1.96 is a fact about the bell curve: it is where the normal distribution leaves 2.5% in each tail. Session 12, prediction intervals, builds them exactly this way.
    • What a failure does. If the residuals are skewed or heavy-tailed, the same arithmetic still runs, but the stated 95% is no longer guaranteed.
    • How to check. The histogram in the residual panels, then a QQ plot, both later today.
    Why it is the forgiving one. If only normality fails, R can build the interval without the bell curve: it simulates many possible futures by reusing the model's own past residuals, drawn at random, and keeps the middle 95%. That is a bootstrapped interval. It still needs constant variance, so a variance failure stays the harder problem. Outside this course's scope; just know it exists.

    A point forecast can be genuinely good while the interval around it is a fiction. Reporting the first without checking the second is where forecasts mislead people.

    A QQ plot, later today on the QQ plots and boxplots slide, puts the residual quantiles against the quantiles of a normal distribution: points on the line means normal, and systematic curvature at the ends means heavy tails.

    Why "no longer guaranteed" rather than "wrong": with heavy tails the 95% interval can come out roughly right by luck while the 99% one is far too narrow, and with skew the misses all land on one side. Either way the coverage is no longer the number on the label.

    For the curious: in fable the bootstrapped version is one argument, forecast(h = 8, bootstrap = TRUE), and fpp3 5.5 covers it under "Prediction intervals from bootstrapped residuals". It also needs the residuals to be uncorrelated, but that is property 1, which you would fix first anyway. Not examined.

    09_B › Example 1 › Reading the four properties

    Bricks, naive and mean

    Read them in the order of the four properties, not the order they are drawn.

    • ACF plot first. It is the finding that changes what you do next. Bars well outside the bounds mean the model is not finished.
    • Then the mean. Does the top panel sit on zero, or is it mostly above or mostly below? That is bias, and it is cheap to fix.
    • Then spread and shape. Does the top panel funnel? Is the histogram symmetric with reasonable tails?
    Both models on bricks fail property 1 badly. That is the expected result, and it is the honest state of a benchmark on a series with real structure in it.

    A failing diagnostic is not a failed exercise. It is the instrument telling you a benchmark is a benchmark.

    Quarterly brick production has a strong season and a long trend, and neither the naive nor the mean model represents either of them. So the seasonal structure walks straight through into the residuals, and the ACF plot shows it at the seasonal lags. That is exactly what you would predict from Session 8's table of which method suits which series.

    Worth trying afterwards on your own: fit SNAIVE() to the same series and run the same diagnostic. The seasonal spikes largely disappear and the trend remains. Watching a diagnostic improve when you pick a better-matched model is the fastest way to learn to read one.
    09_B › Example 1 › QQ plot and boxplot

    Now we compute

    Two plots for the two properties the three panels only half-answer.

    • QQ plot for normality. Your sorted residuals (vertical axis) against where they would sit if they were exactly normal (horizontal axis). On the line means normal; curving away at both ends means heavy tails.
    • Boxplots by year for constant variance. Boxes of similar height mean the spread is stable. A steady climb in box height is a funnel you might have missed.
    • Neither replaces the three panels. They sharpen them: the QQ plot sharpens the histogram, the boxplots sharpen the time plot. The panels tell you whether to look harder; these tell you what you are looking at.

    New to QQ plots? fpp3 does not cover them. Read the Definition and construction and Interpretation sections of Wikipedia's Q–Q plot article before Session 17, which uses them again.

    Four properties, four questions, and no single plot answers all of them. That is why the one-page summary sheet, 09_A in this folder, exists.

    What the two axes hold. The vertical axis is your residuals, sorted from smallest to largest: the sample quantiles, labeled "sample" on the plot. The horizontal axis is where those same points would sit if they came from a normal distribution, meaning the Gaussian bell curve: the theoretical quantiles of a standard normal, labeled "theoretical". They are fixed values, not random draws. If your residuals really are normal the two agree up to scale and the points fall on a straight line; the line's slope and intercept only reflect the residuals' spread and center. Deviation at the ends is the usual finding: ends bending away from the line in opposite directions, an S shape, means heavier tails than normal, and both ends bending the same way means skew.

    fpp3 checks normality with the histogram alone and does not cover QQ plots. The Wikipedia article is the reference, sections Definition and construction and Interpretation.

    The boxplot-by-year construction is the same index_by() thinking from Session 4, used for a different purpose: group the residuals into calendar blocks and compare the blocks.

    The problem with reading bars

    Checking twenty bars is running twenty tests, and about one will fail by chance.

    • Session 4 warned you about this and gave you a visual rule: look for structure, not for a single tall bar.
    • Today you get the proper fix. One test that asks about the first l autocorrelations together, instead of l separate tests.

    A portmanteau test, from the French for a rack that carries several items of clothing. One question, many lags.

    The multiple-comparisons problem is not specific to time series, and meeting it here is a good opportunity to recognize it elsewhere. Any time somebody runs many tests and reports the one that came out significant, this is the mechanism that should make you suspicious.

    Note what the portmanteau test does not do: it will not tell you which lag carries the structure. It answers "is there autocorrelation anywhere in the first l lags", and if you want to know where, you go back to the ACF plot. The two tools are complements.

    Two statistics, the same idea

    Add up the squared autocorrelations. If the total is large, something is there.

    Box-Pierce Q = T · sum over k=1..l of r_k² Ljung-Box Q* = T(T+2) · sum over k=1..l of r_k² / (T − k) l = how many lags to include T = number of observations r_k = the autocorrelation at lag k
    • Both grow with the autocorrelation, because every term is rk squared: never negative, so nothing cancels.
    • H₀: the residuals are white noise. A small p-value rejects it, which means structure is left and the model is not finished.
    • Use Ljung-Box. Box-Pierce is the older, simpler version; the extra weighting makes Ljung-Box better behaved on the short series you will actually have.

    Read the p-value, not the statistic. The statistic has no meaning on its own scale.

    Two arguments to choose. lag is l: fpp3 suggests 10 for non-seasonal data and 2m for seasonal. dof is the number of parameters the model estimated: one for the mean model (the mean) and one for drift, none for naive and seasonal naive. tidy() on the fitted model lists them.

    Why dof matters at all: a model with more parameters can fit noise, so its residuals look more like white noise than they should. Subtracting the degrees of freedom is the correction for that, and it will matter properly once ETS models arrive in Session 19 with several parameters each.

    The detail that makes it work

    Squaring punishes small correlations far harder than large ones.

    r r² shrunk by 0.9 0.81 10% 0.7 0.49 30% 0.5 0.25 50% 0.25 0.06 75% 0.1 0.01 90%
    • Every rk is between −1 and 1, so squaring shrinks all of them. But it shrinks the small ones almost to nothing.
    • A correlation of 0.1 contributes 0.01. One of 0.9 contributes 0.81, eighty times as much.
    • So accidental little correlations cannot add up to a rejection, while genuine structure at a few lags dominates the sum.

    The same trick appears everywhere in statistics, from variance to least squares. Squaring is how you make a sum notice the big things and forgive the small ones.

    Squaring does a second job here that is easy to overlook: it removes the sign. A strong negative autocorrelation is just as much evidence of structure as a strong positive one (Session 4's anti-phase panels are the picture of that), and without squaring the two would cancel in the sum.

    The extra T(T+2)/(T-k) weighting in Ljung-Box gives more weight to the higher lags, where fewer pairs are available and the estimates are noisier. It is a small-sample correction, and on a long series the two statistics agree closely.
    09_B › Portmanteau tests › Running the test

    Now we compute

    features(.innov, ljung_box, lag, dof)

    bricks_aug |> filter(.model == "Nv") |> features(.innov, ljung_box, lag = 8, dof = 0) bricks_aug |> filter(.model == "Mean") |> features(.innov, ljung_box, lag = 8, dof = 1) # .model lb_stat lb_pvalue # Nv 206.0 0 # Mean 269.5 0
    • Both p-values are zero to the printed precision. Reject H₀ comfortably: neither benchmark has captured the structure in this series.
    • Which is the right answer. A naive model on strongly seasonal quarterly data should fail this, and a diagnostic that let it pass would be the broken thing.
    • lag = 8 because the data is quarterly, so 2m = 8. dof counts the parameters the model estimated: none for naive, one (the mean) for the mean model. tidy() lists them.
    • Run it on .innov, not .resid. Identical today, and the habit is what you want before Session 11.

    You now have the full instrument. Session 13 asks the other question: not whether structure is left, but how large the errors are.

    Reading a p-value of 0 in the output does not mean the probability is exactly zero; it means it is smaller than the print precision. Say "we reject at any conventional level" rather than "the probability is zero".

    A caution that will matter in Group Assignment 2. Passing Ljung-Box is not proof that residuals are white noise; it is a failure to detect otherwise, on a test that is weak for short series. Session 4 made the same point about the visual bounds, and it applies with equal force here. "I could not detect remaining structure" is the honest phrasing.

    The 09_A summary sheet, condensed

    Each failure has a different answer. Knowing which is the skill.

    AutocorrelationThe model missed usable structure.Richer model. Hard. Sessions 15–19.
    Non-zero meanForecasts are biased by a constant.Subtract it. Easy.
    Changing varianceOften a multiplicative series fitted additively.Transform. Session 11.
    Not normalThe interval arithmetic does not hold.Transform, or bootstrap the intervals.
    • Only the first one questions the model itself. The other three are about presentation and arithmetic, not about whether the forecast is well aimed.
    • None of them is a reason to hide the diagnostic. A reported failure you cannot fix is a legitimate finding, and Session 7 made the same argument about decompositions.

    Keep the 09_A summary sheet beside you for Group Assignment 2, printed or open in the browser. It is the most used page in the course.

    The third row is worth pausing on, because it closes a loop. A funnel-shaped residual plot usually means the series is multiplicative and you fitted it additively, which is Session 5's very first question back four sessions later with a consequence attached. The eye test you learned for choosing a scheme is the same eye test that reads this plot.

    The fourth row is the most survivable. Bootstrapped prediction intervals need residuals that are uncorrelated with constant variance, but make no normality assumption at all, so a normality failure limits which method you use rather than whether you can quote an interval.
