    Why a whole session on graphs

    Every model in this course is a guess at a shape.

    • Decomposition assumes there is a trend and a season to pull apart.
    • Seasonal naïve assumes the season repeats at a fixed length.
    • Holt–Winters assumes both, and asks you whether the season is additive or multiplicative.

    Each of those is a question a plot answers in four seconds and a model cannot answer at all. So we plot first, always.

    Each of those three methods assumes a shape in the data before it does anything useful. Decomposition assumes a trend and a season exist to be separated. Seasonal naïve assumes the season repeats at a fixed length. Holt–Winters assumes both and then asks whether the seasonal effect adds or multiplies.

    None of those assumptions can be checked by the method itself. All of them are visible in a plot in a few seconds.

    Four questions. Same four, every series, all term.

    1 · Level

    Is it drifting up or down over the long run? That is trend.

    2 · Calendar

    Is there a pattern locked to the calendar, repeating at a fixed length? That is seasonality.

    3 · Waves

    Are there rises and falls that are not locked to the calendar? Those are cycles.

    4 · Damage

    Gaps, spikes, a level that jumps, a variance that grows. Anything that will break a model.

    Answer them in this order. Question 4 is the one that saves you a weekend.

    These four questions are the spine of the session and of the homework. They are worth writing somewhere you will see them again.

    Question 4 does not appear in fpp3 as a separate step, and it is the one practitioners actually do first.

    Three shapes. Learn to name them out loud.

    Trend and seasonality are easy. Everything you will get wrong is the difference between the last two.

    A real series usually shows two or three of these at the same time, layered on top of each other. Separating them is exactly what decomposition does, and that starts in Session 5.

    Trend and seasonality are the easy two. Almost every mistake made in this part of the course is a confusion between the last two.

    The test that settles it

    Seasonal or cyclic? Measure the gaps.

    If you can name the period, it is seasonal. If you can only name a range, it is a cycle.

    The test is mechanical: find consecutive peaks and measure the gap between them. Constant gaps mean the calendar is driving the pattern. Varying gaps mean something else is.

    fpp3 phrases this as “not of fixed frequency”, which is much easier to remember once you have measured the gaps with your own eyes.

    Three differences, and only three

    Same table you will see in the exam.

    LengthSeasonal: constant, and known in advance from the calendar.Cyclic: variable, and only knowable after the fact.
    DurationSeasonal: shorter. One calendar unit up from the sampling interval.Cyclic: longer. Usually at least two years.
    MagnitudeSeasonal: fairly stable from one repetition to the next.Cyclic: varies a lot. Some cycles barely register, some dominate.

    Length is the one that decides it. The other two are supporting evidence.

    Length is the row that decides it. Duration and magnitude are supporting evidence, useful when the length is ambiguous.

    Annual data cannot be seasonal. Ever.

    Those long waves in the lynx data are cycles, not seasons. Annual data admits trend and cycles, never seasonality.

    This one is worth arguing with until it sticks, because the lynx series looks extremely regular and the instinct is to call it seasonal.

    Seasonality is variation within the calendar year, caused by calendar effects. Sample once per year and you have sampled at the same point on every turn of the calendar, so there is no within-year variation left to observe. Regular-looking is not the same as calendar-driven.

    Half-hourly electricity, two regions

    A series shows the seasonality it has, not the seasonality it could have.

    Sub-daily data can carry daily, weekly and yearly seasonality. Whether it does is an empirical question, and the plot is how you ask it.

    Sub-daily data can carry daily, weekly and yearly seasonality all at once. Whether it actually does is an empirical question about that particular series, and the plot is how you ask it.

    The failure mode to avoid: deciding a period from theory and never checking. That is how a weekly term ends up in a model whose data has no weekly pattern.

    US treasury bills, 100 trading days

    That is not a trend. It is the side of a cycle.

    • One hundred days of data. It falls, more or less steadily, all the way through.
    • Zoom out to twenty years and the same stretch is one flank of a long, slow wave.

    How far you can forecast is set by how much of the pattern your window contains. If you have not seen a turn, you cannot forecast one.

    There is no figure on this slide on purpose. The point is about what the window does not contain.

    The practical rule: how far you can forecast is limited by how much of the pattern your data actually covers. If you have never observed a turning point, you have no basis for predicting one.

    03_A  ·  gg_subseries()

    One mini plot per season, side by side.

    Use the subseries plot when the question is about one season over time. It is the only one of the two that separates growth from seasonal growth.

    The horizontal bar in each panel is the mean for that season, and it is the feature most people miss.

    It is what lets you say “Q4 is not merely high, it is pulling away from the others”, which is a different and much more useful claim than “sales are growing”. That distinction is the reason decomposition exists.

    03_A  ·  scatterplots

    Drop the time axis. Keep the pairing.

    • Two time plots stacked, demand above temperature, sharing an x-axis. You can see they move together.
    • Now plot one against the other. Every point is one half-hour: this temperature, that demand.
    • Fit two lines through it. geom_smooth(method = "lm") for the straight one, "loess" for the honest one.

    The straight line goes up gently. The loess is a deep U. Same points, and only one of them is telling you the truth.

    Build it in that order and watch what happens. The straight line rises gently and suggests a weak relationship. The loess curve shows a deep U.

    The U has a physical explanation: heating at the cold end of the range, air conditioning at the hot end, and minimum demand somewhere comfortable in between.

    Two data sets. Both have r = 0.

    r = 0 does not mean no relationship. It means no straight line. Those are very different claims.

    Keep this pair. The left panel is generated from y = x², so the relationship is exact, deterministic and perfectly knowable. The right panel is noise. Both report the same correlation.

    Run cor() on the left panel and you will get something like 1.49e-16 rather than a clean zero. An exponent of minus sixteen is zero for every practical purpose, and recognising that on sight is a useful habit.

    Anscombe's quartet

    Four data sets. One correlation coefficient.

    A correlation coefficient is a summary, and every summary throws something away. Plot it or you are guessing.

    Anscombe constructed these in 1973 and they have not been improved on. All four share the same correlation, the same regression line, the same means and the same variances.

    The fourth panel is the one to remember: a single point is doing all the work. That shape turns up constantly in business data with one dominant customer.

    03_A  ·  back to the electricity

    r = 0.26

    What the number saysA weak relationship between temperature and demand. Barely worth modeling.

    What the plot saysA deep, clean U. Temperature is one of the strongest drivers in the series.

    Heating at one end, air conditioning at the other. The two effects pull the straight line in opposite directions and it comes out nearly flat.

    Two bad but popular readings to be ready for: “r near zero means the variables are unrelated,” and “r near zero means they are independent.” Both are wrong, and you will meet both online.

    The number said weak. The picture said strong. The straight line is nearly flat because the two physical effects pull it in opposite directions and cancel.

    A feature-selection step that drops everything below some correlation threshold would have thrown temperature out of an electricity demand model.

    Two common misreadings to be ready for: that a correlation near zero means the variables are unrelated, and that it means they are independent. Both are wrong.

    Southern Oscillation Index and fish recruitment

    The useful variable is the one that moves first.

    • Recruitment against SOI today: a relationship, but a modest one.
    • Against SOI five, six, seven and eight months ago: stronger every time.
    • So SOI leads recruitment. Recruitment lags SOI. The Pacific changes pressure, and the fish arrive half a year later.

    A driver that moves at the same time as your target is interesting. A driver that moves first is a forecast.

    The business version is the same shape: marketing spend in March and sales in June, a price change and the churn that follows it, hiring and the revenue it eventually delivers.
