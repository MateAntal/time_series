    The vocabulary for the next three sessions

    A series is three components. Trend-cycle is one of them, not two.

    Some books split trend from cycle. We do not, for a reason: no algorithm in this course can separate them, so pretending otherwise would be naming something we cannot compute.

    The cycle is the component Session 3 made you promise not to call seasonality. Seasonality has a fixed, known period tied to the calendar. A cycle has neither: business cycles run somewhere between two and ten years and no two are the same length. Averaging over a fixed window cannot isolate a pattern with no fixed length, which is exactly why the two arrive joined.

    Keep the notation. yₜ, Sₜ, Tₜ and Rₜ are used unchanged in Sessions 6, 7 and 9, in fpp3 chapter 3, and in Group Assignment 1.

    The only question today asks

    Do the three components add up, or multiply together?

    # additive y_t = S_t + T_t + R_t # multiplicative y_t = S_t × T_t × R_t
    • Additive means the seasonal swing keeps its size while the level of the series moves. December adds 40,000 jobs, whether the base is 300,000 or 900,000.
    • Multiplicative means the swing scales with the level. December adds 12%, so it is worth more in absolute terms when the series is high.
    • The choice is yours to make, and every function you meet in this block asks for it. Get it wrong and the components are wrong, silently.

    One symbol between plus and times, and everything downstream depends on it. That is what this session is for.

    The word “scheme” is not a property of the data you can look up. It is a modelling decision you make about the data, and reasonable analysts sometimes disagree about the same series.

    Notice what multiplicative does to the units. In an additive scheme every component is in the units of the series, so Sₜ for retail employment is a number of people. In a multiplicative scheme Sₜ and Rₜ are dimensionless factors that hover around 1, and only Tₜ keeps the original units. A seasonal factor of 1.12 means December runs 12 percent above trend. If you find a multiplicative seasonal component printing values near zero, you have almost certainly fitted the wrong scheme.

    How to tell, from the time plot alone

    Measure the swing early. Measure it late. Compare.

    The test is about the band, not the line. Cover the trend with your hand and ask only whether the gap between the two dashed envelopes is opening.

    Two honest cautions about the eye test.

    First, a series can be short enough that the level barely moves, in which case the two schemes are indistinguishable and either will do. That is not a failure. It is the correct conclusion. Say so rather than inventing a preference.

    Second, the test needs the level to move for a reason other than the season. If the only thing changing is the seasonal amplitude, with a flat trend underneath, you are looking at a series whose seasonality is changing over time. Neither classical scheme handles that well, and it is one of the arguments for STL in Session 7.

    Why a multiplicative pattern is the normal case in business data: spending, sales and headcount tend to grow by percentages rather than by fixed amounts. A retailer whose December is 30 percent above trend keeps that 30 percent as the business doubles. The absolute December bump doubles with it, and the band fans out.

    Why anyone bothers with a transformation

    Take logs and the multiplication becomes addition.

    log( S_t × T_t × R_t ) = log S_t + log T_t + log R_t
    • This is one line of school algebra, and it is the reason transformations appear in a decomposition chapter at all.
    • It does not hide the multiplicative structure. It converts it into a structure that the additive machinery can handle without modification.
    • You pay for it on the way back. Components come out in log units, and interpreting them means undoing the log. That is Session 12's problem, not today's.

    Take logs, and a multiplicative series behaves like an additive one.

    The same identity explains why a multiplicative seasonal factor of 1.12 and an additive seasonal effect of log(1.12) = 0.113 are the same statement. On a log scale, a constant percentage becomes a constant amount, which is exactly the flat band the eye test is looking for.

    One restriction to remember: log() is undefined at zero and below. A series with zeros or negative values cannot be logged, which rules out the transformation for anything that can go negative, such as profits or temperature in Celsius. The inverse transformation on the next slide has the same problem, and worse behaviour near zero.

    05_A  ·  four transformations, increasing strength

    The log is one rung on a ladder. There are gentler and harsher ones.

    • Each rung pulls harder on the large values than the last. Strength means how much it squashes the top of the series relative to the bottom.
    • You are aiming for a flat band, not for the top rung. Overshoot and the band fans out the other way, narrow at the end and wide at the start.
    • The log is the default for a reason. It is strong enough for most business series, and it is the only rung whose components you can read back as percentages.

    Choosing a rung by eye is guesswork with a good track record. Session 11 replaces the guess with a number.

    The inverse transformation is written -1/x rather than 1/x so that it keeps the order of the data: without the minus sign, large values become small ones and the series turns upside down. Every rung on this ladder preserves order, which is what makes them comparable.

    These four are the visible rungs of a continuous family. The parameter that slides between them, and the method for choosing it from the data rather than by eye, is Session 11. Naming it now would not help. You would be choosing a number before you have seen what breaks without one.

    Automating the choice

    Looking at the picture is the right first move. It is not the only one available.

    • The problem is volume. A retailer forecasting every product in every store has tens of thousands of series. Nobody is going to eyeball them.
    • Rule one: decompose both ways and check the leftovers. Run the correlogram on each remainder. The scheme that leaves less structure behind is the better description.
    • Rule two: fit both and compare a fit score. Fit an additive model and a multiplicative one, compare their AIC, and keep the winner.

    Both rules turn a judgement into a computation, and both need machinery you do not have yet. Session 9 supplies the first, Session 19 the second.

    Rule one is the more useful idea to carry forward, because it generalizes past this question. Whenever a model claims to have explained something, run the diagnostic on what it failed to explain. If structure survives in the remainder, the model missed it. That is the entire logic of residual diagnostics in Session 9, and Session 4's white-noise test is the tool it uses.

    AIC is a fit score with a penalty for complexity, so it will not simply prefer whichever model has more moving parts. You meet it properly in Session 19, where it chooses among the ETS family. Today it is enough to know that such a number exists and that the comparison is mechanical.

    Detrended and seasonally adjusted

    Once you have the components, remove one and see what is left.

    Additive   yₜ = Sₜ + Tₜ + RₜMultiplicative   yₜ = Sₜ × Tₜ × Rₜ
    Detrended Dₜy - trend   leaves Sₜ + Rₜy / trend   leaves Sₜ × Rₜ
    Seas. adj. Aₜy - seasonal   leaves Tₜ + Rₜy / seasonal   leaves Tₜ × Rₜ
    • The operation follows the scheme, with no exceptions. Additive removes by subtracting, multiplicative removes by dividing.
    • Getting it wrong is silent. Subtracting a seasonal factor of 1.12 from a cost of 3.5 runs without a warning and returns a number that means nothing.
    • Neither series is the trend. Both still carry Rₜ, which is the point of the next slide.

    Four cells, one rule. This table is the whole of tonight's homework, applied twice.

    Read the right-hand column in words and it stops being arithmetic to memorize. A multiplicative seasonal component is a factor: December is 1.12 times trend. Removing it means dividing by 1.12, which is what “taking the December effect out” means when the effect is a percentage.

    A sanity check you can run in one line: after a multiplicative seasonal adjustment, the adjusted series should sit close to the original in level. If yours is suddenly a hundred times larger or a hundredth the size, you subtracted where you should have divided, or divided by the wrong component.

    The mistake this slide exists to prevent

    The seasonally adjusted series still carries the remainder. It is not smooth.

    To read direction, use the trend-cycle component. Use the seasonally adjusted series when the seasonal pattern is what is in your way, not when smoothness is what you want.

    This is why unemployment figures are published seasonally adjusted rather than raw: a January rise should read as a change in the economy, not as the end of the Christmas hiring season. The adjustment answers the question “is this more than the usual January?”

    And it is also why commentary on those figures so often goes wrong. A one-month move in a seasonally adjusted series is mostly remainder, and reading a recession into it is the mistake this slide is about. Wait for the trend estimate instead, which has the cost you would expect: the trend at the most recent month is the hardest part of a decomposition to pin down, and Sessions 6 and 7 will show you why the ends of a trend estimate are missing altogether.

    The tool for the check is the subject of the next three slides. It is one function, it behaves in a way that surprises almost everybody the first time, and ten minutes well spent now beats a silent wrong answer in Session 7.

    Before the right tool, the wrong one

    0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 is FALSE, and R is not wrong.

    > 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 [1] FALSE > print(0.1 + 0.2, digits = 20) [1] 0.30000000000000004441
    • None of those three numbers exists exactly in binary. Each is stored as the nearest representable value, and the errors do not cancel.
    • == asks for exact equality, element by element, and returns a vector of TRUE and FALSE. On decimals it reports differences that are not there.
    • Your two columns will differ like this. R computed season_adjust by a different route from yours, so the last few digits will not match.

    The comparison you want is not “identical”. It is “the same to a tolerance you can defend”.

    A double-precision number carries about sixteen significant decimal digits, so the gap in the printout above sits at the seventeenth. It is not a rounding error you introduced, and no amount of care in your own code removes it. Every language that uses binary floating point behaves this way, which is why this trap has its own well-known name in every one of them.

    identical() does not rescue you either. It is stricter than ==, not looser: it compares type as well as value, so identical(1L, 1) is FALSE because one is an integer and the other a double. Useful for asking “are these the same object”. Useless for asking “is my arithmetic right”.

    The one function to get right today

    all.equal() returns TRUE, or a sentence. It never returns FALSE.

    • It does not compare element by element. That is what == does. all.equal() collapses the whole comparison into one verdict.
    • It allows a tolerance, about 1.5e-8 by default: wide enough to swallow floating-point noise, narrow enough to catch a real mistake.
    • So a failed check does not look like a failed check. It looks like a line of text, and R is perfectly happy to print it and move on.

    Two possible return types from one function. That is unusual, it is deliberate, and it is where the next slide's bug comes from.

    The design makes sense once you see what it is for. all.equal() was written for testing, where a bare FALSE tells you nothing useful. “Mean relative difference: 0.1” tells you how far apart the objects are, and it will report other kinds of mismatch in the same way: all.equal(1:3, 1:4) returns "Numeric: lengths (3, 4) differ".

    The default tolerance is sqrt(.Machine$double.eps), roughly 1.5e-8, which is about half the significant digits a double carries. The reasoning is that errors accumulate through a calculation, so demanding agreement to the last bit would fail on correct code. You can pass a different tolerance if you need one, but on this course you will not.

    Making the result safe to act on

    isTRUE(all.equal(x, y))

    > if (all.equal(1, 1.5)) "same" else "different" Error: argument is not interpretable as logical > isTRUE(all.equal(1, 1.5)) [1] FALSE > isTRUE(all.equal(0.1 + 0.2, 0.3)) [1] TRUE
    • A sentence is not a condition, so if (all.equal(x, y)) stops with an error the moment the two disagree. It works while your code is right and breaks when it is wrong.
    • isTRUE() turns the character string into FALSE and leaves TRUE untouched. One wrapper, and the result is an honest logical.
    • You do not need round(). Absorbing floating-point noise is what the tolerance is for. Reaching for round() to make a check pass means reaching for the wrong function.

    Write it once by hand tonight and it stays written. You will use it in every remaining session of this block.

    Four tools, four jobs, worth keeping straight. == compares element by element and gives you a vector, which is what you want for filtering rows. identical() asks whether two objects are the same in every respect including type. all.equal() asks whether two numeric objects agree within a tolerance, and explains itself when they do not. isTRUE(all.equal(...)) is that same question with an answer you can put in an if.

    The error message is a fair warning rather than a silent failure, which is the one break this trap gives you. Where it does bite silently is inside a larger expression: a filter, a vectorized condition, anything that coerces rather than checks. So make the isTRUE() wrapper a habit rather than something you add when an error appears.

    When neither label fits

    Some series are multiplicative in the season and additive in the noise.

    y_t = ( S_t × T_t ) + R_t
    • The seasonal swing grows with the level, so season and trend multiply. That half of the series is multiplicative.
    • The noise does not grow with the level, so it is added on top. Think of a measurement error fixed by the instrument, not by the size of what is measured.
    • Neither pure scheme describes it well, and the pure log trick does not fix it, because a log cannot separate an added term from a multiplied one.

    Additive and multiplicative are the two ends of a range, not a pair of boxes every series falls into. Session 11 gives you the tool for the middle.

    A concrete case: a sensor reading a quantity that grows by percentages, where the sensor's own error is a fixed number of units regardless of the reading. The signal is multiplicative, the error is additive, and the two combine as the formula above.

    Do not go looking for mixed schemes in tonight's homework. Both worked examples are clean cases, chosen for that reason. This slide exists so that when you meet a series that stubbornly refuses both labels, you know it is a known situation with a name rather than a failure of your eye.
