    Session 1 closed on the observation that neighbouring observations in a time series are related to each other, and left it unresolved on purpose. That is the thread this session picks up.

    The idea the whole session rests on

    You are looking at one draw.

    Your data is a sample of size one from the process you actually care about. That is the whole difficulty of this course.

    This is the hardest idea in the session and it is worth sitting with. The series you were handed is not the behaviour of the thing you are studying. It is one outcome that the underlying mechanism happened to produce, and it did not have to come out this way. A different draw of the weather, the competitor who almost launched in March, which customers happened to churn that quarter, and the curve would sit differently on the page, even with everything else about the business held fixed.

    That is why a single historical series tells you much less than its length suggests. Ask yourself: was last year's curve the only one that could have happened?

    The one piece of notation you have to keep straight

    Big Y is what could happen. Small y is what did.

    { Y1, Y2, Y3, …, Yt }

    The processRandom variables, indexed by time. This is the object we build models of. It is never printed in a table.

    { y1, y2, y3, …, yt }

    The realizationNumbers. One specific outcome of that process. This is your CSV.

    Every confusing exam question in this course is really asking which of these two columns you are standing in.

    Framing from Shumway & Stoffer, Time Series: A Data Analysis Approach Using R, Chapter 1. It is not in fpp3 at all.

    This notation is standard across the time series literature and you will meet it in every textbook, so it is worth getting comfortable with early. The convention is not decorative: uppercase and lowercase refer to genuinely different objects, and mixing them up is the source of a lot of confusion later.

    “What did” means two things at once, not two different things: y_t is both the realized value and one particular instantiation out of everything Y_t could have been. They coincide because, once a moment has passed, the instantiation that happened is the realization — the randomness resolved into that one number and stopped being random. The two readings only pull apart in a simulation, where you can draw a value without any time passing at all.

    The framing comes from Shumway & Stoffer, Time Series: A Data Analysis Approach Using R, Chapter 1. It is not covered in fpp3, so the textbook will not help you here.

    The past is a number. The future is still a random variable.

    Forecasting is describing a random variable that has not happened yet. That is where the interval comes from.

    T marks the last observed time, and h is the horizon: how many steps beyond it you are forecasting. This is the same h you will type all term in forecast(h = 8), and it matches fpp3's own notation for a forecast, ŷ_{T+h|T} — read as “the forecast of y at time T+h, given what we know up to T.”

    Notice that the distributions get wider the further right they sit. That is not an accident of the drawing: uncertainty grows with the forecast horizon, and quantifying exactly how it grows is the subject of Session 12.

    A question worth asking yourself: was the past ever random? Yes, before it happened. It stopped being random at the moment it was realised, which is precisely what separates the two halves of this picture.

    Two pictures. Only one of them is a time series.

    Not independent. Not identically distributed. The Central Limit Theorem has nothing left to stand on.

    Two separate assumptions fail here, not one.

    Independence fails because each observation carries information about the next. Identical distribution fails because the mean and the variance move over time. Most people remember the first and forget the second, and the second is what makes trends and changing volatility possible at all.

    The dashed line in each panel is the mean: flat on the left, drifting on the right.

    Where the correlation comes from

    Because yesterday causes today.

    • Yesterday's closing inventory is this morning's opening stock.
    • Last month's subscribers are most of this month's renewals.
    • This morning's temperature is most of this afternoon's.

    The correlation is not contamination in the data. It is the only reason forecasting works at all.

    It is tempting to file autocorrelation under “assumption violation” and treat it as damage. It is the opposite. If today carried no information about tomorrow there would be nothing to forecast and no need for this field.

    Session 4 gives this property a name and a way to measure it.

    The bill for the last six slides

    Four things you already own, and what they cost now.

    Standard errors

    The familiar σ/√n assumed independent draws. With correlated data it is wrong, usually too small.

    The CLT

    Its hypothesis is independent, identically distributed observations. You cannot appeal to it here.

    k-fold CV

    Shuffling rows destroys the ordering, which is the thing you are modeling. Session 14 replaces it.

    A forecast

    Stops being a number. Becomes a distribution, with an interval you have to report.

    Three losses and one gain. The rest of this course is rebuilding the three, and learning to use the one.

    Each of these losses is repaired later in the course, and the session that does it is named on the card. Nothing here is being taken away permanently.

    Why the familiar standard error is wrong under correlation is genuinely beyond this course, so do not chase it. It is enough to know that it is wrong, and usually too small.

    From the concept to the code

    A variable indexed by time needs a table that knows about time.

    • A data frame has rows. It does not know which column is the clock.
    • It does not know whether two rows are the same series or two different ones.
    • It will let you hold two observations for the same day, for the same series, and say nothing.

    Every time series bug I have had to debug in fifteen years was one of those three. A tsibble makes all three impossible.

    These three failures sound abstract until they cost you a day. A plain data frame will happily let you sort a series into the wrong order, mix two series together, or hold two observations for the same date, and it will report no error in any of those cases.

    The tsibble makes all three impossible by construction, which is the entire reason the course uses it.

    02_A  ·  types

    Use the smallest type that does the job.

    <date> 2017-01-31 days since 1970-01-01 <time> 20:11:59 seconds since midnight <dttm> 2017-01-31 20:11:59 seconds since 1970-01-01, plus a time zone

    A dateOne integer. No time zone. Nothing to get wrong.

    A date-timeDrags a time zone with it, and therefore drags daylight saving with it.

    If daily data answers your question, do not store it hourly. Precision you do not need is only a place for bugs to live.

    The practical consequence: a date-time carries a time zone, and a time zone carries daylight saving. Any series crossing a daylight-saving boundary has one 23-hour day and one 25-hour day a year, which is a real source of confusion in half-hourly energy data.

    vic_elec, which appears in Session 3, is stored in Australia/Melbourne for exactly that reason.

    02_A  ·  from a string

    You name the order. lubridate does the parsing.

    ymd("2017-01-31") → 2017-01-31 mdy("January 31st, 2017") → 2017-01-31 dmy("31-Jan-2017") → 2017-01-31 ymd(20170131) → 2017-01-31

    Read your source. Write down the order the components appear in. That spelling is the function name.

    Four wildly different strings, one line of code each, no format string anywhere.

    The rule is mechanical. Read your source, write down the order the year, month and day appear in, and that spelling is the function you need. No format strings, no guessing.

    02_A  ·  the one to remember

    Same eight characters. Three different days.

    parse_date("01/02/15", format = "%m/%d/%y") → 2015-01-02 parse_date("01/02/15", format = "%d/%m/%y") → 2015-02-01 parse_date("01/02/15", format = "%y/%m/%d") → 2001-02-15

    R cannot tell which one you meant. Neither can Excel, which is why it silently guesses using the locale of whoever opened the file.

    A date column that parsed without an error is not a date column that parsed correctly. Check the first row and the last row against the source, every time.

    This is the most expensive slide in the session measured in careers. A Spanish analyst and an American analyst open the same export and build two different series, and neither of them sees a warning.

    The habit that protects you: after parsing a date column, check the first row and the last row against the source file by eye. Every time.

    02_A  ·  types from tsibble

    Three types that carry the calendar with them.

    yearmonth(ymd("2017-01-31")) → 2017 Jan [1M] yearquarter(ymd("2017-01-31")) → 2017 Q1 [1Q] yearweek(ymd("2017-01-31")) → 2017 W05 [1W]

    Not base RThese come from tsibble, loaded by fpp3. They will not exist in a plain R session.

    Why botherA <date> knows it is the 31st. A <yearmonth> knows it is January, and that January comes round again.

    Storing the period, not just the instant, is what makes next session's seasonal plots possible.

    A <date> knows it is the 31st of January. A <yearmonth> knows it is January, and that January comes round again next year. Storing the period rather than just the instant is what makes the seasonal plots of Session 3 possible.

    Try it: build a monthly series with a plain <date> index and watch the tsibble report [1D] with gaps. Convert the index to yearmonth and it becomes [1M].

    Open 02_B_tsibbles

    Index, key, and everything else.

    You are not adding data. You are telling R something it could never have worked out on its own.

    You are not adding information to the table. You are declaring structure that R could never have worked out on its own: which column is the clock, and which columns tell one series from another.

    02_B  ·  the two lines you read first

    The header is the whole contract.

    # A tsibble: 15,150 x 9 [1Y] # Key: Country [263]

    [1Y]The interval. One observation per year. R worked this out from the type of the index column.

    Country [263]The key. 263 separate series stacked in one table.

    Read these two lines before you read a single row. They tell you what you have and they are almost always where the mistake is.

    Read these two lines before you read a single row of data. They tell you what you are holding, and they are where a surprising number of mistakes become visible.

    The error is not the tsibble being awkward. It is the tsibble catching a duplicate that you would otherwise have averaged over without ever noticing.

    02_B  ·  nycflights13

    Sometimes it takes two columns to name one thing.

    flights %>% mutate(time = make_datetime(year, month, day, hour, minute)) %>% as_tsibble(key = c(carrier, flight), index = time) # A tsibble: 336,776 x 4 [1m] <UTC> # Key: carrier, flight [5,725]

    Flight 2900 exists at more than one airline. The number alone does not identify a series, any more than a first name identifies a person.

    Ask what uniquely names one series in your data, then make that the key. Guessing costs you an hour.

    Flight 2900 exists at more than one airline, so the flight number alone does not identify a series, any more than a first name identifies a person.

    The business version of this is the same shape: a product code may not be unique across stores, and a store code may not be unique across countries. Ask what uniquely names one series before you write the key.

    02_B  ·  index_by()

    A tsibble will not quietly throw time away.

    The dangerous one is on the left. It did not error. It answered a question you never asked.

    The version on the left is the dangerous one, and it is worth understanding why. It did not throw an error. It returned 127,328 rows of a perfectly well-formed answer to a question nobody asked.

    Silent wrong answers are much harder to catch than loud failures. index_by() is group_by() that understands the clock.

    What to reach for

    Three verbs cover almost everything.

    Buildtsibble() from vectors, as_tsibble() from a tibbleAlways name index =. Name key = if you have more than one series.
    Subsetfilter(), exactly as in dplyrRows go away, the index and key survive. Nothing special to learn.
    Aggregateindex_by() then summarise()Whenever the result is coarser in time than the input. Never group_by().

    If you ever find yourself casting to a tibble and back, stop. There is an index_by that does it properly.

    You will find, in the notebook and online, a workaround that casts to a tibble, aggregates, and casts back. It works, and on a table with more than one series it will silently drop the key. Prefer index_by().
