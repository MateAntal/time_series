    Where we are

    Seven sessions describing series you already had. Now you predict one you do not.

    • Everything so far was description. Graphs, correlograms, decompositions. All of it stops where the data stops.
    • Session 4 named the bar. White noise is the null hypothesis of this course, and every model from today on is a claim to beat something.
    • Today you build the something. Four forecasting methods so simple you can compute them in your head.

    Not because they are good. Because a clever model that cannot beat them has not earned its complexity, and you need to be able to prove that.

    The benchmark idea is older and broader than forecasting. Any time somebody shows you a model, the first question worth asking is what it is being compared against, and the second is whether that comparison was fixed in advance.

    There is a real professional habit in this. A model that beats nothing has not been shown to work; it has only been shown to run. The four methods on the next slide are the ones the whole field agrees to measure against, which is what makes them worth an entire session.

    fpp3 5.2  ·  the whole session in four shapes

    Each one answers "what happens next?" with a different one-line rule.

    Seasonal naive is the one that surprises people. On a strongly seasonal series it is hard to beat, and papers get published failing to beat it.

    The dashed green line under the drift panel is worth noticing now, because slide 10 is about it: the drift slope is not fitted by least squares. It is the straight line through the first and last observation, and nothing in between affects it.

    Naive has a deeper significance than its simplicity suggests. If a series is a random walk, then the naive forecast is the optimal forecast, and no amount of modelling will beat it. That is the claim behind the efficient market hypothesis, which is Group Assignment 2 in Session 17.

    One picture for the whole course

    Two different things get called "the model's output".

    Residual and forecast error are different quantities measured on different data. Session 13 is built entirely on keeping them apart.

    Keep this picture. It is the single most reused diagram in the course: Session 9 works on the blue gaps, Sessions 13 and 14 work on the orange ones, and every model from Session 15 on produces both.

    The reason the distinction matters so much in practice is that residuals are always available and forecast errors are not. You can compute a residual the moment you fit a model; you have to wait for the world to happen before you can compute a forecast error. A model chosen on residuals alone is a model chosen on the easier question.

    Read the subscript, not the hat

    The bar in the subscript tells you what the model was allowed to know.

    # forecast ŷ_{T+h | T} the value at T+h, given everything up to T # fitted value ŷ_{t | t-1} the value at t, given everything up to t-1
    • Left of the bar: when. Right of the bar: what information was available. That reading works for every model in this course.
    • A fitted value is a one-step-ahead forecast, made on data the model has already seen, for a point it was not allowed to look at.
    • That is what makes a residual honest. If the fitted value could see yt, the residual would measure memorization rather than error.

    Both get written ŷt when nobody is being careful. The book does it, this course does it, and the subscript is where you recover the meaning.

    The vertical bar is read "given", and it is the same bar as in conditional probability. That is not a coincidence: yT+h|T really is a conditional random variable, conditioned on the information available at time T. Slide 13 takes that seriously.

    A small notational warning for later. Once transformations arrive in Session 11 you will meet ŵt|t-1 as well, the fitted value in the transformed domain. Same reading, different units.

    And the one method that breaks the rule

    Three differences, and one exception worth knowing.

    Forecast   ŷT+h|TFitted value   ŷt|t-1
    WhereBeyond the data, after TInside the data, t ≤ T
    How farAny horizon hAlways one step
    Compared withA future observation → forecast errorThe observation at t → residual
    The mean model breaks this. Its fitted value at t is the average of the whole series, so it uses observations from after t. It is not a one-step-ahead forecast, and it is the only benchmark today that is not.
    • Which makes its residuals flattering. The model saw the answer, in the sense that yt is one of the values it averaged.
    • The definition still holds for the other three, and for everything from Session 15 onward. Exceptions get named when they arrive.

    Worth carrying into Session 9: a diagnostic run on residuals is only as honest as the fitted values underneath it.

    How much the mean model actually gains from this is small when T is large, because one observation out of several hundred barely moves the average. On a very short series it is not negligible at all.

    You will meet the same idea in a much sharper form in Session 14. Cross-validation exists precisely to stop a model being judged on data it was fitted to, and the mean model here is the mildest possible version of that problem.

    Two columns that are identical today

    .resid is in the units of the data. .innov is in the units the model worked in.

    # no transformation: the two are the same number .resid = y_t - ŷ_t # with a transformation w_t = log(y_t), the model fits w, not y .innov = w_t - ŵ_t <- the innovation residual .resid = y_t - ŷ_t <- back in the original units
    • Nothing is transformed today, so the two columns are equal and you can check that with isTRUE(all.equal(.resid, .innov)).
    • They part company in Session 11, when transformations arrive, and that is when the distinction starts to cost you if you ignore it.
    • Session 9 works on .innov. Diagnostics belong in the space the model actually fitted in, not the space you happen to report in.

    You do not need the distinction yet. You need to know the column exists, so that the day it stops being a duplicate you notice.

    "Innovation" is the standard term for the part of an observation that the model could not have anticipated. It is the same word used for the error term in a state space model, which is where the ETS family in Session 19 gets its formal footing.

    augment() is the function that hands you all of this: the original series, .fitted, .resid and .innov, one row per observation per model. It is worth running once on a mable with several models in it just to see the shape of what comes back.

    fpp3 5.2  ·  the two flat ones

    Both forecast a constant. They disagree about which constant.

    MEAN(y) ŷ_{T+h|T} = (y_1 + y_2 + ... + y_T) / T # the average NAIVE(y) ŷ_{T+h|T} = y_T # the last value
    • Mean uses everything equally. An observation from thirty years ago counts as much as last month's, which is either the right assumption or a very wrong one.
    • Naive uses one number and throws away the rest. That sounds reckless and is the optimal forecast for a random walk.
    • Naive's fitted value is lag(y). Literally the previous observation, so you can check the whole .fitted column against a Session 4 one-liner.

    Which one is right depends on whether the series has a stable level to return to. That is a question about the data, and you already know how to answer it.

    Stock prices are the classic case where naive wins and mean loses badly: there is no long-run average price to revert to, so the best guess for tomorrow really is today's close. Group Assignment 2 in Session 17 is built on exactly this.

    The opposite case is a stable physical process with a fixed operating point, where the mean is hard to beat and the naive forecast simply propagates whatever noise happened to land on the last observation.

    The one that is hard to beat

    Every future March is the last March you actually saw.

    SNAIVE(y) ŷ_{T+h|T} = y_{T+h-m(k+1)} m = the seasonal period k = the whole number of seasons between T and T+h
    • The formula looks worse than the idea. All the k is doing is finding the most recent observation that sits in the same position of the season.
    • Its fitted value at t is yt-m, so the first m fitted values are missing. Same reason the moving average had gaps in Session 6: nothing to look back at yet.
    • It carries the whole seasonal shape for free, without estimating a single parameter. On a strongly seasonal series that is a genuinely high bar.

    If a forecasting paper does not report seasonal naive as a comparison on seasonal data, be suspicious. It is the benchmark most often quietly omitted.

    Read the formula once with numbers and it stops being frightening. Monthly data, m = 12. For h from 1 to 12, k = 0, so the forecast is yT+h-12: the matching month of the last complete year. For h from 13 to 24, k = 1, so it is yT+h-24, the same twelve values again. The forecast simply repeats.

    SNAIVE() takes an optional lag argument, which you only need when a series has more than one plausible seasonal period and you want to choose. The half-hourly electricity data from Session 3 is the case where that matters.

    Naive, allowed to slope

    The drift line is drawn through two points. Only two.

    Equivalent to drawing a line through the first and last observations and extending it. Say that out loud and the formula stops needing to be memorized.

    The telescoping is worth doing once on paper. The average change is the sum of (yt − yt-1) from t = 2 to T, divided by T − 1. Expand the sum and everything cancels except yT − y1. The script computes both forms and checks they agree.

    In fable the drift method is written RW(y ~ drift()), where RW stands for random walk: a naive forecast is a random walk with no drift, and this is the same model with a drift term added. tidy() will show you the estimated drift, and it is the only one of today's four methods with a parameter to estimate at all.

    Now we compute

    model() → augment() → forecast()

    fit <- employed |> model( Mean = MEAN(Employed), Naive = NAIVE(Employed), SNaive = SNAIVE(Employed), Drift = RW(Employed ~ drift()) ) fit |> augment() # .fitted, .resid, .innov fit |> forecast(h = 24) # 24 months beyond the data
    • Four models in one call. The result is a mable, a model table, with one column per model.
    • This shape does not change for the rest of the course. Session 15 onward swaps what goes inside model() and nothing else.
    One library. fpp3, no data files. Every series today ships in the package.
    Learning the workflow on methods this simple is deliberate. When ETS() arrives in Session 19 you will have enough to think about without also learning what augment() returns.

    One trap the script warns about: select() on a mable drops the key columns, so fit |> select(Drift) |> forecast() produces forecasts that autoplot() then refuses to draw against the data. Filter the fable instead of selecting from the mable.

    08_A  ·  on the RStudio screen

    Check a fitted value by hand before you trust a single plot.

    • Naive. Is .fitted the previous observation? Compare it against lag(Exports) and confirm with isTRUE(all.equal(...)).
    • Seasonal naive. How many rows at the top have no fitted value, and does that number equal m? Predict before you look.
    • Drift. Does tidy()'s estimate match (yT − y1) / (T − 1) computed by hand?

    Same habit as the decomposition block, on a new object. You are checking that the function does what the formula says, on a case where you can still tell.

    All three checks pass, and the script runs them. The point is not the suspense, it is that these are the last models in the course whose output you can verify in your head. From Session 15 on you will be taking the function's word for it, and the habit you want by then is one you built here.

    The .model column is how augment() keeps four models' worth of rows apart in one table. filter(.model == "Drift") is how you get at one of them, and the same column appears in the fable that forecast() returns.

    fpp3 5.3  ·  what the shaded fan means

    The number is the middle of a distribution, not the answer.

    • Each future point is a random variable, not yet realized. The past is a set of random variables that already collapsed to numbers.
    • The point forecast is the mean of that distribution. It is what .mean holds, and it is the least interesting thing the model produces.
    • The fan widens because it should. Uncertainty compounds with horizon, and a forecast plotted without it is a forecast that overclaims.

    This is Session 2's stochastic-process framing paying off. A time series is a collection of random variables indexed by time, and a forecast is a statement about the ones that have not happened yet.

    Drawing the intervals is one line: autoplot() includes them by default, and level = NULL is what turns them off. Turning them off is the right choice when you are comparing four methods' shapes on one plot, and the wrong choice when you are reporting a result to somebody who will act on it.

    Where the interval widths come from is Session 12, and the answer depends on the residuals behaving themselves, which is Session 9. That is the honest dependency chain: you cannot trust an interval until you have checked the residuals it was built from.

    08_A  ·  on the RStudio screen

    Four methods, one plot, and an argument about which is least wrong.

    • Plot all four with level = NULL. Four shapes on one set of axes is the clearest picture of what each method assumes.
    • Then plot one of them with its intervals. The difference in how confident the two pictures look is the point of the exercise.
    • Ask which one you would defend. Not which is correct — there is no way to know that yet. Which one you would defend, and from what feature of the series.
    Measuring which is actually better is Session 13. Today you argue from the shape of the series. That is a legitimate first move, and it is not a substitute for the measurement.
    On US private employment from 2010, the series trends upward with a clear annual season, so seasonal naive and drift are both defensible and mean is plainly wrong. Say why: the mean forecast sits below every observation of the last several years, because it is averaging over a period the series has already left.

    That failure is a good one to look at properly. The mean model is not broken; it is answering the question "what is the typical value of this series" faithfully, on a series where that question has no useful answer. Choosing a benchmark is choosing which question you think the data can answer.

    fpp3 5.7  ·  the decomposition block, closed

    Split the series, forecast the pieces separately, put it back together.

    This is what makes the decomposition block a forecasting tool rather than a description. Three sessions of machinery, finally pointed forward.

    decomposition_model() does all four steps in one call: you hand it a decomposition specification and one model per component, and it fits, forecasts and recombines. The script shows the syntax.

    One limitation to know. tidy() cannot describe a decomposition_model, because it is two models combined rather than one, and it will tell you so with an error rather than a wrong answer. If you want the parameter estimates, decompose first and fit the two parts separately. The script does that too.

    This approach is the direct ancestor of the STL plus ETS method used throughout the follow-up course, so it is worth more attention than its single section in the book suggests.

    08_A  ·  on the RStudio screen

    One call, two models inside it.

    employed |> model( stl_drift = decomposition_model( STL(Employed), # how to split RW(season_adjust ~ drift()), # the non-seasonal part SNAIVE(season_year) # the seasonal part ) ) |> forecast(h = 24) |> autoplot(employed)
    • Compare it against plain drift on the same series. The seasonal shape is back, and the trend is still there.
    • The column names are not arbitrary. season_adjust and season_year come out of the STL decomposition, which is why you had to meet them in Session 7.

    Everything inside that call is from the last three weeks. The only new thing is the wrapper that holds them together.

    Try swapping RW(season_adjust ~ drift()) for NAIVE(season_adjust) and watch the forecast go flat while keeping its seasonal shape. That one substitution shows you exactly which part of the final forecast each inner model is responsible for, and it takes ten seconds.

    From Session 15 the inner model on the seasonally adjusted part becomes an exponential smoothing method rather than a benchmark, and the same wrapper still holds. That is the shape worth remembering.

    The practical question

    Match the method to what the series actually does.

    No trend, no seasonThe series wanders around a stable level.MEAN
    No stable levelA random walk. Nothing to revert to.NAIVE
    Strong seasonA repeating pattern that dominates the picture.SNAIVE
    Steady trendA consistent direction over the whole history.DRIFT
    Trend and seasonBoth at once, which none of the four handles.Decomposition
    • Every row is a question you can answer from a time plot, and you have been answering them since Session 3.
    • Report more than one anyway. A benchmark you chose after seeing which one won is not a benchmark.

    Session 13 replaces this table with a measurement. Until then, arguing from the shape of the series is the honest method, as long as you say that is what you did.

    The last row is the one that makes today's final section matter. All four benchmarks are either flat, straight, or seasonal, and none is both sloping and seasonal. A series with trend and seasonality is exactly the case that needs the decomposition route.

    The warning about choosing after the fact is not pedantry. Fixing your comparison before you see the results is what makes the comparison mean anything, and it is the same discipline that makes a pre-registered experiment worth more than an exploratory one.
